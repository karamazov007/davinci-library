Staying in the loop

The Company Grapevine

I’m in the office only a day or two a week — usually one — and the friend who was my ear on the ground has left. Without an insider I’m flying blind on what’s happening: what’s hot, how review cycles work, what increments look like, who’s rising. This page is a plan to cultivate a reliable person I can talk to and ask, so I’m never out of the loop again.

The problem
Out of the loop is a real cost

One office day a week and no insider means I hear things late or never — and get blindsided at appraisal time or miss quiet opportunities. A single trusted, in-the-flow ally flips that: information comes to me early and honestly, and I can plan instead of react.

What to learn
The intel I actually want from them

Not gossip for its own sake — specific, decision-useful signal: when review cycles happen and what they reward, what the average increment/hike looks like, what’s currently hot and prioritised, re-orgs and who’s rising, the unwritten norms, and where projects are really headed.

The takeaway

One well-chosen, well-tended relationship replaces a whole information network. The rest of this page is how to pick that person, build the bond, keep a cadence I can sustain, and talk in a way that makes them want to share.

The right person

Who to Pick

The relationship is only as good as the person. You want someone in the flow of information, who enjoys sharing, keeps a confidence, and isn’t competing with you for the same rung.

The filter
Choose for reach + trust, not just friendliness

The best pick is often someone you already get on with who happens to be well-networked and long-tenured — start from existing warmth. Avoid the office gossip (they’ll leak you too), anyone as out-of-loop as you, and direct rivals for your role or rating.

The takeaway

Pick one or two people, not a crowd. Depth of trust beats breadth — a single person who tells you the truth is worth more than ten who tell you nothing.

Earning the relationship

Build the Bond

Connection is assembled, not lucky — even on one day a week. Supply the ingredients and turn the reciprocity loop, and a genuine two-way tie forms. (Same machinery as the Understanding Humans → Bonds section.)

The recipe
Give first, be safe, keep it two-way

On your office day, be present and consistent; give before you take (share something useful, grab a coffee, help with a small thing); show real interest in them, not just their information; be a little open yourself so it isn’t pure extraction; and above all be discreet — a vault. Trust compounds each cycle.

The takeaway

Never make them feel used. The person should feel they gain a warm, discreet, reciprocal friend — the information is a by-product of a real relationship, not the point of it.

How often

Cadence & Rhythm

The whole thing lives or dies on rhythm. With one office day a week, the trick is to make that day count in person and keep a light, steady thread going between visits — never bursts followed by silence.

The weekly beat
Deep in person + light async

Go the same day they’re in so you actually overlap, and spend real time together — lunch, coffee, a walk. Between visits, keep it warm with 1–2 low-stakes pings on Google Chat (a relevant link, a “how did X go?”, a small favour) — not a firehose. Warmth decays with silence, so a small steady minimum beats occasional floods.

Same office day = overlapLunch / coffee / walk-and-talk1–2 Google Chat pings/weekGive-value pings, not extract-onlySteady > sporadic

The takeaway

Aim for both office days if you can; if it’s one, make it their day and protect a shared meal. Consistency is the signal — showing up predictably is what turns an acquaintance into an ally.

The conversation

How to Talk & What to Ask

How you ask decides whether they open up or clam up. Make it natural, mutual and unhurried — give a nugget, ask an open question, listen, react warmly, remember. Never interrogate, never leak.

The manner
Make it safe and two-way

Lead with something you know or a genuine question about them; ask open (“how’s the review cycle looking this time?”) not rapid-fire; and guard everything they tell you. Repeat their confidences once and the tap closes for good.

Review cycle
  • When does the next cycle actually kick off?
  • What are they really weighting this time?
  • Who decides my rating — manager, skip, committee?
  • What separated top-rated people last cycle?
Increments & hikes
  • What’s a typical increment for my band?
  • What did strong performers actually get?
  • Any freeze, budget squeeze, or bump this year?
  • Is a counter/negotiation normal here?
What’s hot
  • Which teams/projects have leadership’s attention?
  • What’s getting funded vs quietly wound down?
  • Where’s the growth — and where’s the risk?
Org & norms
  • Any re-orgs or leadership changes brewing?
  • Who’s genuinely rising, and why?
  • Unwritten rules — how things really get done here?

The takeaway

Ask for signal, not secrets. Keep it a friendly two-way exchange, protect their trust like your own, and over time you’ll always know what’s coming before it arrives.

Be seen before the hike

The Hike Project Play

Quiet, steady work doesn’t get rewarded if nobody sees it. In each quarter of the hike cycle — especially the last one or two — ship one high-impact, visible project that higher management can point to. That’s what actually moves your number.

Timing
Back-load your biggest, most visible win

Appraisals lean on recent, visible memory. A brilliant thing you shipped in Q1 fades; a solid win in the final quarter is fresh in the room when ratings are set. So pace yourself to peak late — land your flagship, most-visible project in the last quarter or two before the hike.

What counts
What makes a project a “dope” project

Impact and visibility are different things — you need both. Solve something leadership already worries about, be the clear owner, put a number on the result, and then make sure the deciders actually watch it land. A great project nobody sees doesn’t count at appraisal.

1 flagship per quarterSolve what leadership cares aboutOwn it end-to-endPut a number on itMake it visible upwardPeak in the final quarter

The takeaway

Build something undeniable — then make sure the people who set your number actually see it, ideally right before the cycle closes. Impact that’s invisible, or old news by review time, quietly gets forgotten.

Borrowed experience

Court the Veterans

People who’ve been here 6–10 years hold a map you don’t have yet — how cycles really work, who matters, where things are heading. Even as a digital engineer, cultivating a couple of long-tenured seniors and higher-ups buys you perspective now and sponsorship later.

Why them
A veteran carries things you can’t shortcut

The biggest prize is the green one: sponsorship — a senior who speaks for you in rooms you’re not in. That only comes after real relationship. Everything else (memory, network, reading the cycles, knowing the unwritten rules) you get to borrow along the way.

The approach
Ask for their story, not for favours

The opener that always works: “How did you build your career here — what would you do differently?” People love being asked for their story and their wisdom. Come curious, be useful in small ways, and never make the first ask a favour. The mentorship — and later, the sponsorship — grows from there.

Their journey
  • How did you get to where you are here?
  • What decisions worked — and what would you redo?
  • What did you wish someone told you early?
Reading the company
  • How has this place changed over your years?
  • What actually gets people rated & promoted here?
  • Where do you see things heading next?
My growth
  • As a digital engineer, where should I aim next?
  • What skills compound most in this org?
  • Would you be open to me checking in now and then?
Light mentorship ask
  • Mind if I run a decision past you sometimes?
  • Who else here is worth learning from?
  • Anything I could help you with in return?

The takeaway

You’re not asking for a leg-up — you’re earning a mentor. Ask for their story and their advice, listen hard, be useful, and sponsorship follows the people who genuinely value what veterans know.

Close the loop on your work

How Did the Client Meeting Go?

When you build something but a CBM member or lead presents it to the client, you’re blind to the most important signal — how it landed. Ask them. You get product feedback money can’t buy, and you deepen the bond with the person who fronts your work.

The gap
You build, someone else presents — and the signal never returns

The default flow drops you out of the one moment that matters — the client’s real reaction. The dashed red arrow is the feedback you never receive. One question to the presenter (“how did the client take it?”) closes that loop and puts you back in the flow.

Did it land?
  • How did the client take it — first reaction?
  • What did they like most?
  • Where did they push back or hesitate?
Make my work better
  • Anything confusing or missing in what I built?
  • What would’ve made your pitch easier?
  • Any follow-ups or changes they want from me?

The takeaway

Ask “how did the meeting go?” every single time. You get feedback that sharpens your work — and the CBM presenter becomes an ally who’ll champion what you build instead of just relaying it.

From professional to personal

Climb the Closeness Pyramid

With your manager it’s perfectly professional right now — all work, the odd small aside, rarely a joke. On the spectrum from perfectly-professional to perfectly-casual, the aim is to nudge steadily toward casual — one rung at a time, mostly in your 1:1s and the free moments.

The spectrum
You’re near the left — move rightward, gently

You don’t leap from professional to casual — you slide. Each 1:1 is a chance to move one notch rightward: a little more warmth, a shared laugh, a personal aside. Overshooting feels forced; a steady drift feels natural.

The ladder
The pyramid of closeness — climb rung by rung

Most manager relationships sit at rungs 1–2 forever. The move you want is 3 and up — where banter, real rapport and a bit of trust live. You don’t need “best friends”; even reaching a comfortable rung 3–4 changes how your 1:1s feel and how she advocates for you.

The how
Small moves that lift you a rung — in a 1:1 or a free moment

Concrete moves, not a personality transplant: open with one personal line about yourself, ask a real question about her world, risk a light joke, notice a shared interest and pull that thread, remember what she told you last time, and do the occasional tiny kindness. Any one of these lifts a conversation a rung.

One personal thread per 1:1Ask about her worldRisk a light jokeMine a shared interestRemember & follow upClimb, don’t leap

The takeaway

Add one personal thread to every 1:1 — a weekend question, a shared interest, one joke. Don’t lunge for casual; climb a single rung and let it settle. Warmth compounds, and a manager who knows you as a person advocates for you differently.

Turn your network into income

The Referral Pipeline

Openings appear at your company constantly — after every review cycle and randomly in between. Each successful referral pays roughly ₹1,00,000. So keep a warm, tagged network of people looking to move, and when a fitting role drops, you match, refer, and earn. You’re the matchmaker sitting between supply and demand.

The mechanism
A two-sided market — and you’re the broker

The bonus exists because hiring is expensive and trusted referrals convert better than cold applicants — the company pays you for lowering its hiring risk. Your leverage is the structural hole: you know good people the company can’t easily reach, and you know openings those people can’t easily see.

The funnel
Volume and quality in, bonus out

Because the funnel narrows hard, two things matter most: a wide, warm top (lots of people who’ll pick up your call) and speed + fit at the match step — referrals are competitive, so being early with the right person beats being late or spraying weak ones.

The supply side
Build a living, tagged candidate list

Keep a simple private list (a sheet or notes) of people who are open to moving, tagged so you can instantly filter when a role appears. The tags that matter: what they do, how senior, how soon they can join, how keen they are, and location/remote fit.

The operating loop
A rhythm that keeps the pipeline alive

The engine is step 1 and step 4: keep helping people regardless of openings (they’ll say yes when you have a match), and only refer those you’d genuinely vouch for. A couple of great referrals make recruiters trust your name; a bad one poisons it.

Keep a tagged candidate listKnow the referral policy & payout termsWatch openings via the grapevineMatch fast — be earlyVouch only for people you rateHelp even when there’s no role

The takeaway

Treat it as a quality pipeline, not a spray. Maintain a warm, tagged network of job-seekers, stay wired into openings, and move fast with people you’d stake your name on. Each clean match is ~₹1 lakh and a reputation as the person who brings in good people — which pays far beyond the bonus.

The first principle

The Root Fact

Everything about office politics grows from one fact: your value is real but invisible. Nobody can measure it directly, so decisions get made on proxies — reputation, visibility, who vouches for you, how you make people feel. Value has to be transmitted through people to count.

The core diagram
The transmission gap

Because nobody can read your mind or audit your worth, they lean on signals. That isn’t unfair — it’s unavoidable under incomplete information. So the task is never just “be good”; it’s get your realness across the wall so the perception matches the value.

The takeaway

You don’t have a competence problem, you have a transmission problem. Every tactic on this page is one move repeated: shrink the gap between how good you are and how good the deciding humans know you are.

Where it comes from

Why Politics Exists

Politics isn’t a flaw some companies have and others don’t. Put three conditions together — interdependence, scarcity, ambiguity — and an informal layer of influence appears automatically to settle what authority and metrics can’t.

The trigger
Three conditions → politics is guaranteed

Every workplace has all three. That’s why every workplace has politics. Seeing it as the system’s informal layer — not a dirty add-on — is what lets you play it with clear eyes instead of pretending it isn’t there while others use it.

Chart vs network
Who can decide vs who actually does

Authority is only part of the story. Real outcomes often get settled by connectors and brokers — people with influence far beyond their title. Learning to read the second picture, not just the first, is most of “political skill.”

The takeaway

Don’t moralise politics as good or bad — read it. It’s the predictable result of humans deciding scarce things under uncertainty. Map the informal network and you’re no longer playing blind.

What you’re paid in

The Two Currencies

You’re always earning two currencies at once: competence (can you do the work) and trust/relationship (do people want to work with you, vouch for you, protect you). Competence is necessary but never sufficient — it only converts into advancement through the second.

The map
Where competence alone lands you

The trap is the bottom-right box: excellent work, no backing — so you stall while less capable but better-connected people move. The only move is up: keep the competence, add the trust and visibility that make people vouch for you.

Conversion
Competence doesn’t pay out on its own

Think of relationship + visibility as the gearbox. Competence is engine power, but without the gearbox it never reaches the wheels. Most stalled careers are all engine, no transmission.

The takeaway

Bank both currencies deliberately. If you’re strong on competence, your highest-leverage work isn’t more competence — it’s the trust and visibility that let it pay out.

The whole literature

The Two Schools

The literature splits into two honest camps — one says be generous and authentic, the other says merit isn’t auto-rewarded, so build real power. Both are right about something. The truth is the synthesis, with network science underneath both.

The spectrum
Idealist ↔ realist — and the synthesis

Don’t pick a side. Run the generous, reputation-building long game (idealist) and stay clear-eyed that visibility, networks and sponsorship are what convert competence into advancement (realist). The two aren’t opposites — they’re both true at once.

The network law
Value comes from bridging groups that don’t talk

Your strongest ties (your own team) mostly know what you already know. The new information — and the leverage — comes from weak ties to other groups, and from being the person who bridges them. That’s why breadth of relationship, not just depth, pays.

The takeaway

Read one from each camp (Grant’s Give and Take, Pfeffer’s 7 Rules of Power) and hold both. Then build a network that bridges, not just one that runs deep inside your own team.

The line that matters

Positive vs Negative Politics

Same skills, two directions. Positive politics builds trust, visibility and alliances for mutual benefit — it compounds. Negative politics — manipulation, credit-theft, coalitions against people — wins once, then wrecks you in the repeated game. Aim to be trusted and visible.

The trajectories
Only one compounds over time

Manipulation can spike your standing for a quarter. But work is a game you replay with the same people, and a betrayed trust never fully returns — so the red line craters. Reputation is the compounding asset; protect it like capital.

The test
Positive vs negative, side by side

A clean gut-check for any move: does it create value for others as well as me, or does it only take? The first builds a reputation that opens doors for years; the second buys a short lead and a long shadow.

The takeaway

Be the rare person who is both deeply trusted and highly visible. That combination is scarce, compounds for years, and is completely available to you without a single dishonest move.

How it all fits

The Operating System

The insider you cultivate here is one node in a bigger machine. I Know a Guy, the Ladder of Closeness, the Comms Lifecycle and the Favor Bank are one system for turning a stranger into a reliable tie — assess, get in, climb, bank, maintain.

The whole system
Five notes, one machine

The pieces you've built aren't five separate ideas — they're one pipeline for turning a stranger into a reliable tie. You assess the person, get in with the Comms Lifecycle, climb the Ladder of Closeness as far as the spark allows, bank favours, and maintain the handful that matter.

Step 0 — assess first
Is this a hub, an ally, or just keep-warm?

Before you spend energy, place the person. Two axes: how connected they are (reach) and how much trust the tie can carry. A hub earns the reach track; a trusted ally (your grapevine insider) earns the deep track; someone you simply meet a lot you just keep warm at the base; and the rare person high on both gets the full investment.

Two tracks, one engine
Reach vs reliability — same machinery, different depth

The two targets need different things — a hub gives you reach, an ally gives you reliability — but you build both with the same tools: the Comms Lifecycle to get in, the Ladder of Closeness to go deep. You just climb higher with the ally and stay broader with the hub.

The shared depth mechanism
The Ladder of Closeness — climb only with the few

Depth is one ladder for everyone: open at the base with anyone (the plan, the food, "which team?"), step up to work & company for shared ground, to interests where the spark lives, and to the personal top only with your handful. Climb a rung only when you feel a spark — most ties stay near the base, and that's correct.

Cue — Assess cold, connect warm. Place the person (hub / ally / keep-warm), run the Lifecycle to get in, climb the Ladder as the spark allows, and bank favours you'll maintain. A node can be both a hub and an ally, and people migrate — so keep re-reading. Give like an "otherish" giver: generous but boundaried.

The takeaway

This is one system, not five notes. For anyone worth it: work out whether they're a hub (reach) or a trusted ally (reliability), use the Comms Lifecycle to get in, climb the Ladder of Closeness as far as the spark allows, and build a Favor Bank you keep alive. Same machinery, tuned to the node — and the way to make it real is reps: quizzes, case questions, and roleplay.

The highest-leverage move

Build an App

Build your own application and you cut through the two hardest things at once: visibility to the people above you, and connection to a company that's full of developers. One project, both wins.

Why it's the move
One build → two of the hardest things at once

Most people have to choose where to spend effort — get seen, or get connected. A build does both from one artefact: it's a visible, demoable thing to show leadership, and it's instant common ground with the hundreds of engineers around you.

What building teaches you
You start speaking the developer's language

Building forces you through the real developer experience — tokens and APIs, backend and frontend, inspecting the page, hunting bugs, solving your way out. You don't need to become an engineer; you need to speak the language, because that fluency is what makes both the meetings and the hallway conversations land.

Path 1 · visibility
Build → ask seniors → demo upward

The build gives you a legitimate reason to reach up: you have real questions and a real thing to show. Asking senior people for feedback flatters and informs at once, gets you into meetings, and ends with you demoing your work to the people who set your rating.

Path 2 · connection
A company full of devs → instant common ground

A company of developers speaks one native language: building. Your app is the perfect excuse to talk to any of them — ask how they'd have done it, trade a bug story, get a code review. That's how a build quietly turns strangers into the hubs and allies from the network playbook.

Cue — Pick one small, real app and build it end-to-end. Let the doubts you hit become reasons to reach senior people; demo the result upward. Then use the same app as common ground to talk shop with the developers all around you. One project → visibility and network.
Ship one small real appLearn the dev's world — tokens · APIs · debugAsk seniors for feedbackDemo it upward → meetingsCommon ground with 100s of devsOne move, two wins

The takeaway

Building an app is the highest-leverage single move in this whole page: it makes your competence visible to the people who decide, and gives you instant common ground with a company full of engineers. It's the Hike Project and the network play, fused — reach and reliability from one artefact.
