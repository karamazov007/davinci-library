Staying in the loop

The Company Grapevine

I’m in the office only a day or two a week — usually one — and the friend who was my ear on the ground has left. Without an insider I’m flying blind on what’s happening: what’s hot, how review cycles work, what increments look like, who’s rising. This page is a plan to cultivate a reliable person I can talk to and ask, so I’m never out of the loop again.

The problem
Out of the loop is a real cost

One office day a week and no insider means I hear things late or never — and get blindsided at appraisal time or miss quiet opportunities. A single trusted, in-the-flow ally flips that: information comes to me early and honestly, and I can plan instead of react.

What to learn
The intel I actually want from them

Not gossip for its own sake — specific, decision-useful signal: when review cycles happen and what they reward, what the average increment/hike looks like, what’s currently hot and prioritised, re-orgs and who’s rising, the unwritten norms, and where projects are really headed.

The takeaway

One well-chosen, well-tended relationship replaces a whole information network. The rest of this page is how to pick that person, build the bond, keep a cadence I can sustain, and talk in a way that makes them want to share.

The right person

Who to Pick

The relationship is only as good as the person. You want someone in the flow of information, who enjoys sharing, keeps a confidence, and isn’t competing with you for the same rung.

The filter
Choose for reach + trust, not just friendliness

The best pick is often someone you already get on with who happens to be well-networked and long-tenured — start from existing warmth. Avoid the office gossip (they’ll leak you too), anyone as out-of-loop as you, and direct rivals for your role or rating.

The takeaway

Pick one or two people, not a crowd. Depth of trust beats breadth — a single person who tells you the truth is worth more than ten who tell you nothing.

Earning the relationship

Build the Bond

Connection is assembled, not lucky — even on one day a week. Supply the ingredients and turn the reciprocity loop, and a genuine two-way tie forms. (Same machinery as the Understanding Humans → Bonds section.)

The recipe
Give first, be safe, keep it two-way

On your office day, be present and consistent; give before you take (share something useful, grab a coffee, help with a small thing); show real interest in them, not just their information; be a little open yourself so it isn’t pure extraction; and above all be discreet — a vault. Trust compounds each cycle.

The takeaway

Never make them feel used. The person should feel they gain a warm, discreet, reciprocal friend — the information is a by-product of a real relationship, not the point of it.

How often

Cadence & Rhythm

The whole thing lives or dies on rhythm. With one office day a week, the trick is to make that day count in person and keep a light, steady thread going between visits — never bursts followed by silence.

The weekly beat
Deep in person + light async

Go the same day they’re in so you actually overlap, and spend real time together — lunch, coffee, a walk. Between visits, keep it warm with 1–2 low-stakes pings on Google Chat (a relevant link, a “how did X go?”, a small favour) — not a firehose. Warmth decays with silence, so a small steady minimum beats occasional floods.

Same office day = overlapLunch / coffee / walk-and-talk1–2 Google Chat pings/weekGive-value pings, not extract-onlySteady > sporadic

The takeaway

Aim for both office days if you can; if it’s one, make it their day and protect a shared meal. Consistency is the signal — showing up predictably is what turns an acquaintance into an ally.

The conversation

How to Talk & What to Ask

How you ask decides whether they open up or clam up. Make it natural, mutual and unhurried — give a nugget, ask an open question, listen, react warmly, remember. Never interrogate, never leak.

The manner
Make it safe and two-way

Lead with something you know or a genuine question about them; ask open (“how’s the review cycle looking this time?”) not rapid-fire; and guard everything they tell you. Repeat their confidences once and the tap closes for good.

Review cycle
  • When does the next cycle actually kick off?
  • What are they really weighting this time?
  • Who decides my rating — manager, skip, committee?
  • What separated top-rated people last cycle?
Increments & hikes
  • What’s a typical increment for my band?
  • What did strong performers actually get?
  • Any freeze, budget squeeze, or bump this year?
  • Is a counter/negotiation normal here?
What’s hot
  • Which teams/projects have leadership’s attention?
  • What’s getting funded vs quietly wound down?
  • Where’s the growth — and where’s the risk?
Org & norms
  • Any re-orgs or leadership changes brewing?
  • Who’s genuinely rising, and why?
  • Unwritten rules — how things really get done here?

The takeaway

Ask for signal, not secrets. Keep it a friendly two-way exchange, protect their trust like your own, and over time you’ll always know what’s coming before it arrives.

Be seen before the hike

The Hike Project Play

Quiet, steady work doesn’t get rewarded if nobody sees it. In each quarter of the hike cycle — especially the last one or two — ship one high-impact, visible project that higher management can point to. That’s what actually moves your number.

Timing
Back-load your biggest, most visible win

Appraisals lean on recent, visible memory. A brilliant thing you shipped in Q1 fades; a solid win in the final quarter is fresh in the room when ratings are set. So pace yourself to peak late — land your flagship, most-visible project in the last quarter or two before the hike.

What counts
What makes a project a “dope” project

Impact and visibility are different things — you need both. Solve something leadership already worries about, be the clear owner, put a number on the result, and then make sure the deciders actually watch it land. A great project nobody sees doesn’t count at appraisal.

1 flagship per quarterSolve what leadership cares aboutOwn it end-to-endPut a number on itMake it visible upwardPeak in the final quarter

The takeaway

Build something undeniable — then make sure the people who set your number actually see it, ideally right before the cycle closes. Impact that’s invisible, or old news by review time, quietly gets forgotten.

Borrowed experience

Court the Veterans

People who’ve been here 6–10 years hold a map you don’t have yet — how cycles really work, who matters, where things are heading. Even as a digital engineer, cultivating a couple of long-tenured seniors and higher-ups buys you perspective now and sponsorship later.

Why them
A veteran carries things you can’t shortcut

The biggest prize is the green one: sponsorship — a senior who speaks for you in rooms you’re not in. That only comes after real relationship. Everything else (memory, network, reading the cycles, knowing the unwritten rules) you get to borrow along the way.

The approach
Ask for their story, not for favours

The opener that always works: “How did you build your career here — what would you do differently?” People love being asked for their story and their wisdom. Come curious, be useful in small ways, and never make the first ask a favour. The mentorship — and later, the sponsorship — grows from there.

Their journey
  • How did you get to where you are here?
  • What decisions worked — and what would you redo?
  • What did you wish someone told you early?
Reading the company
  • How has this place changed over your years?
  • What actually gets people rated & promoted here?
  • Where do you see things heading next?
My growth
  • As a digital engineer, where should I aim next?
  • What skills compound most in this org?
  • Would you be open to me checking in now and then?
Light mentorship ask
  • Mind if I run a decision past you sometimes?
  • Who else here is worth learning from?
  • Anything I could help you with in return?

The takeaway

You’re not asking for a leg-up — you’re earning a mentor. Ask for their story and their advice, listen hard, be useful, and sponsorship follows the people who genuinely value what veterans know.

Close the loop on your work

How Did the Client Meeting Go?

When you build something but a CBM member or lead presents it to the client, you’re blind to the most important signal — how it landed. Ask them. You get product feedback money can’t buy, and you deepen the bond with the person who fronts your work.

The gap
You build, someone else presents — and the signal never returns

The default flow drops you out of the one moment that matters — the client’s real reaction. The dashed red arrow is the feedback you never receive. One question to the presenter (“how did the client take it?”) closes that loop and puts you back in the flow.

Did it land?
  • How did the client take it — first reaction?
  • What did they like most?
  • Where did they push back or hesitate?
Make my work better
  • Anything confusing or missing in what I built?
  • What would’ve made your pitch easier?
  • Any follow-ups or changes they want from me?

The takeaway

Ask “how did the meeting go?” every single time. You get feedback that sharpens your work — and the CBM presenter becomes an ally who’ll champion what you build instead of just relaying it.

From professional to personal

Climb the Closeness Pyramid

With your manager it’s perfectly professional right now — all work, the odd small aside, rarely a joke. On the spectrum from perfectly-professional to perfectly-casual, the aim is to nudge steadily toward casual — one rung at a time, mostly in your 1:1s and the free moments.

The spectrum
You’re near the left — move rightward, gently

You don’t leap from professional to casual — you slide. Each 1:1 is a chance to move one notch rightward: a little more warmth, a shared laugh, a personal aside. Overshooting feels forced; a steady drift feels natural.

The ladder
The pyramid of closeness — climb rung by rung

Most manager relationships sit at rungs 1–2 forever. The move you want is 3 and up — where banter, real rapport and a bit of trust live. You don’t need “best friends”; even reaching a comfortable rung 3–4 changes how your 1:1s feel and how she advocates for you.

The how
Small moves that lift you a rung — in a 1:1 or a free moment

Concrete moves, not a personality transplant: open with one personal line about yourself, ask a real question about her world, risk a light joke, notice a shared interest and pull that thread, remember what she told you last time, and do the occasional tiny kindness. Any one of these lifts a conversation a rung.

One personal thread per 1:1Ask about her worldRisk a light jokeMine a shared interestRemember & follow upClimb, don’t leap

The takeaway

Add one personal thread to every 1:1 — a weekend question, a shared interest, one joke. Don’t lunge for casual; climb a single rung and let it settle. Warmth compounds, and a manager who knows you as a person advocates for you differently.
