Learn the structure.

A language is mostly sentence structure + word modification + a few caveats. Learn those once; the rest is just words (Google Translate is the engine). We learn Japanese this way — and extract the recipe for any language as we go.

千里の道も一歩から

A journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step.

The approach, in four moves

  • Structure before script. Not starting with hiragana / katakana / kanji — writing is the last phase.
  • Comparative pipeline. English → Hindi → Japanese; Hindi & Japanese are both SOV with heavy particles.
  • Words are a numbers game. Once the skeletons hold, fluency ≈ words × structure.
  • Meta-learning always on. Anything that generalizes gets lifted into the Meta tabs.

Meta · any language

The phase model

The arc for taking on any new language, tested against Japanese.

1

Crack the meta-structure

Month one: sentence structure, word modification, and the core principles — the skeleton, not vocabulary. Done when you can build a correct (if clumsy) sentence and flip it to a question or negative.

2

Word accumulation

Fluency becomes words × structure. Convert words relentlessly and drop them into shapes you already own — Google Translate feeds words into your structures.

3

Conversation

Roleplay and “playwriting” — scripted dialogues acted out to pressure-test structure + words under near-live conditions.

4

Writing the script

Last. Learn to write — especially non-Latin scripts — once you already speak. The script is a coat of paint on knowledge you own.

Meta · any language

The core principles

The month-one checklist for any language — attack these first.

1

Word order

SVO? SOV? VSO? English SVO · Hindi SOV · Japanese SOV. Get this and half the reordering is automatic.

2

Role marking

Roles by position (English), particles (Japanese), or postpositions/case (Hindi)? Decides if word order is rigid or free.

3

Word modification

Tense, aspect, politeness, number, comparison — what bends and how. In Japanese it lands on the tail.

4

Fusion / agglutination

Words glue into one (Japanese) or stay apart with helpers (Hindi)? Knowing the packaging style saves confusion.

5

Caveats & intricacies

High-frequency exceptions that break the clean rules. You can't guess these — you collect them by using the language.

Meta · any language

Generalization log

Findings that turn out to be general — each an axis to ask any new language about.

Position-based vs marker-based roles

English uses word position; Japanese (particles) and Hindi (postpositions) use markers. First question for any new language: which one?

Questions: particle vs inversion

Japanese ka (end) & Hindi kya (front) add a particle; English inverts word order.

Wh-words: in-situ vs fronting

Japanese & Hindi leave the question word in place; English fronts it.

Predicate-final

Japanese & Hindi put the predicate last; English drops it in the middle. Axis: SOV / SVO / VSO?

Meta · how progress is measured

Proficiency & exams

The universal “ranking” for language ability is the CEFR — a six-rung ladder from A1 to C2. Japanese has its own exam ladder, the JLPT (N5 → N1). Here's how both work, and how they line up.

The one idea

CEFR is a ruler, not an exam. It describes what you can do with the language (“can-do” statements), from A1 (survive) to C2 (mastery). Separate exams (IELTS, JLPT, DELF…) then test you and place you on that ruler.

The CEFR ladder — A1 → C2

Six levels in three bands. Numbers go up with skill (A1 lowest → C2 highest).

A1
Breakthrough A · Basic user
Handle basic phrases; introduce yourself; ask/answer simple personal questions — if the other person speaks slowly and helps.
A2
Waystage A · Basic user
Simple, routine exchanges on familiar topics — shopping, family, work basics. ≈ tourist survival.
B1
Threshold B · Independent
Cope with most travel situations; grasp the main points of familiar topics; describe experiences and give short reasons. ≈ “conversational”.
B2
Vantage B · Independent
Follow complex & abstract text; interact with natives fluently, without strain for either side. ≈ work in the language.
C1
Advanced C · Proficient
Fluent and spontaneous; catch implicit meaning; use the language flexibly for academic and professional purposes.
C2
Mastery C · Proficient
Understand virtually everything; express nuance precisely; near-native. (A “Pre-A1” starter level also exists below A1.)

How the JLPT exam works

Three competencies

Language Knowledge (vocabulary + grammar) · Reading · Listening. At N2/N1 the first two are merged into one section.

Scoring

Out of 180. Pass marks: N5 ≥ 80 · N4 ≥ 90 · N3–N1 ≥ 95–100 — and you must clear a minimum (~19) in every section.

When & how

Held twice a year (July & December), paper-based, multiple-choice. Certificates don't expire.

The big catch

JLPT tests only reading & listening — no speaking, no writing. It certifies recognition, not production (the same blind spot as Duolingo).

Read these mappings loosely

JLPT isn't officially aligned to CEFR — these are widely-used approximations (the JF Standard / CEFR-J is the formal bridge). Treat them as “about here,” not exact.

Where we're aiming

You start at A1 / N5. The first big, useful plateau is B1 / N3 — “conversational”, independent. That's a realistic first target; N2 is the job-ready bar. And remember: because JLPT skips speaking/writing, passing it ≠ being able to talk — which is exactly why our method front-loads producing sentences.

Meta · how others teach

The Duolingo curriculum

How the world's biggest language app actually teaches — and why it's almost the exact inverse of our structure-first bet. Understanding the contrast sharpens our own method.

Their bet in one line

Duolingo optimizes for retention through habit: teach breadth of vocabulary & phrases in frequency order, let grammar be absorbed implicitly through repetition and translation — and for Japanese, teach the script first.

The machine (any language)

A strict linear ladder built on the CEFR scale (A1 beginner → B2 upper-intermediate). You walk one path forward — no branching, no back — because clarity of “what's next” keeps people going.

Sectiona CEFR band→ Unita theme · ~10 levels→ Level1+ lessons→ Lesson~17 questions

A lesson mixes translate · read · listen · pronounce, and adapts — do well → harder items; struggle → review. Newer types: audio-only, roleplay, and stories.

Four pedagogy pillars

1

Frequency-first vocab

The commonest words & survival phrases first, grouped by everyday themes (food, family, travel) — not by grammar rules.

2

Implicit grammar

Rules are inferred from examples, not taught. Optional “guidebooks” exist, but grammar is a byproduct, not the spine.

3

Spaced repetition

Words resurface at widening intervals, baked into the forward path — review happens without you choosing it.

4

Gamified daily habit

Streaks, XP, hearts, leagues — engineered so you return for a small daily dose. The habit is the product.

Duolingo vs us — the head-to-head

We are deliberately doing the opposite on almost every axis.

The deep difference

Duolingo is bottom-up accumulation — pour in thousands of words, let grammar precipitate out of repetition. We're top-down — master the small skeleton that generates sentences, then pour words into it. Their well-known weakness is the flip side of their strength: many finish a whole tree and still can't freely produce a sentence, because grammar stayed implicit. Their real superpower: people actually keep going — consistency beats intensity.

What to steal · what to reject

✅ Steal

Spaced repetition for our word-accumulation phase · a daily micro-habit (10 focused min > rare heroics) · the CEFR ladder as a milestone ruler · their roleplay = our Phase 3 (conversation).

🚫 Reject

Script-first — we defer kana on purpose · implicit grammar — you want the why; osmosis is why learners plateau · translation-as-the-whole-method — recognising ≠ producing.

Honest caveat on our side

Structure-first has its own risk — it can feel abstract with no words to use. Duolingo's answer (hand people words & phrases to play with immediately) is worth respecting; it's why our Phase 2 (accumulate words, fast) must follow closely once the skeleton holds.

SRS
Spaced Repetition System. Not an app — a technique. A flashcard method that shows you a card right before you'd forget it, stretching the gap each time you get it right (1 day → 3 days → a week → a month…). The most efficient way to move words into long-term memory.
Sentence mining

The cue

Every sentence is one of three shapes, set by its last word: noun + desu, adjective + desu, or a verb. Two rules govern them all.

Japanese · Quick reference

Cheat codes, tricks & tips

Every high-leverage shortcut in one place — the structural “cheat codes”, the memory tricks for the fiddly bits, and the study hacks that make it stick.

The meta-cheat

Japanese is smaller than English — fewer forms, and it loves dropping what's obvious. Every cheat below is really the same move: let the particle do the work, and say less.

The structural cheat codes

The dozen shortcuts that collapse most of the difficulty.

⚡ Verb always last

Set the scene first; save the verb for the end. If you get stuck, you can even pause before the verb.

⚡ Read the tag, not the position

Particles mark the role, so word order is free. は = topic wherever it sits, を = object wherever it sits.

⚡ Only 3 sentence shapes

The last word decides: noun + です · adjective + です · verb. That's every sentence.

⚡ Drop the pronoun

If it's obvious who, leave “I / you” out. tabemasu = “(I) eat”. Over-using pronouns is the English-brain tell.

⚡ One word + particle

I / me / my / mine = watashi + wa / o / no. No five separate forms to memorise.

⚡ Only two tenses

Non-past & past. No future, no “perfect.” “Will eat” and “eat” are the same word.

⚡ Only two irregular verbs

suru (do) and kuru (come). Every other verb follows a regular ending-swap.

⚡ When unsure, be polite

Default to です / ます — it's never rude. Casual だ/plain comes later.

⚡ Use your Hindi

English → Hindi does the reorder (verb last); Hindi → Japanese just swaps postpositions for particles (का→の…).

⚡ な is just の

na-adjectives behave like nouns: kirei na hana mirrors nihongo no hon.

⚡ Question = + か

Add か at the end. The question word (nani, doko…) stays where the answer goes — never moves to the front.

⚡ Six particles, most sentences

Master の · は · を · に · が · で first — they carry the vast majority of everyday Japanese.

Sound & form tricks

  • Silent u. The final u in です／ます is barely voiced → say “dess”, “mass”.
  • Devoiced i. 好き suki sounds like “ski” (i drops between voiceless sounds).
  • Hold long vowels; pause on doubles. gakkō (学校) has a held “o” and a tiny pause on the double-k.
  • Spot the verb group fast: ends in ‑eru / ‑iru → usually ichidan (taberu, miru); anything else → godan; only suru / kuru are irregular.
  • Learn each adjective's type with the word (i- or na-), like gender in Hindi — don't guess from the sound (trap: kirei, kirai look i- but are na-).

は vs が — the pocket heuristic

Until we do the full lesson

は = known info you're commenting on — could you say “as for X”? Then は. が = the new thing, or the answer to “who / what?” (question-word answers take が). Rough but right most of the time.

Study cheats — how to make it stick

  • Spaced repetition (Anki) — review a word just before you'd forget it. The single biggest memory cheat.
  • Learn words inside sentences, never as bare lists — mine real sentences from what you read/watch.
  • Feed yourself i+1 — input just above your level (you follow ~80–90%). Too hard isn't learning, it's noise.
  • Output early — produce sentences (to Claude), don't only recognise. Producing is where fluency is built.
  • 80/20 — the top ~1,000 words + the 6 core particles carry most of everyday Japanese. Learn the big tiles first.
  • Daily micro-habit > cramming — 15 focused minutes every day beats a rare marathon.
  • Rewatch anime you already know — familiarity makes the Japanese comprehensible.
  • Shadow — repeat audio aloud, copying the rhythm, to fix pronunciation.

The “you” trap — don't say it, name it

Saying anata at people feels blunt or distant. Japanese uses the person's name + さん san where English says “you”.

Don't pointあなた anataअनाता · “you”
→
Use their name田中さん Tanaka-sanतानाका सान · “Mr./Ms. Tanaka”

Other “you” words exist — but skip them

君 kimi किमि (soft, often to juniors) and お前 omae ओमाए (rough, can offend) are real, but the safe move is name + さん san सान. Reserve kimi/omae for reading, not speaking.

The big habit — drop the pronoun

English forces a subject in every sentence. Japanese drops it when context is clear — over-using watashi / anata is the #1 tell of an English-brain speaker.

English — subject is mandatory

“I” must appear, always.

(watashi wa)(वताशि वा)食べます tabemasuतबेमास

食べます tabemasu तबेमास = “(I) eat.” The verb alone is a full sentence.

Rule 1 · how it works

The predicate goes last

English drops the verb into the middle. Japanese (and Hindi) make you wait — the action lands at the very end. Everything before it is setup.

Why it matters

To understand a Japanese sentence you must hold everything until the end — the verb (and its tense/negation) arrives last. Training your ear to “wait for the verb” is half of listening.

So can a noun be the predicate? Yes — via the copula

The last word is always one of three: a verb, an i-adjective, or the copula です／だ (the “is”). A noun can’t stand at the very end bare — it leans on です. So a noun predicate = noun + です, and です is the true final word.

動

Verb

tabemasu 食べます · तबेमास — stands on its own.

形

i-Adjective

takai 高い · तकाई — carries its own “is”.

だ

Copula です／だ

hon desu, kirei desu — closes noun & na-adj predicates.

Where does the “is” sit?

EN this is a book (middle) · HI ye kitaab hai (end) · JP kore wa hon desu (end). Japanese & Hindi park the copula at the very end; English drops it in the middle.

Cue

Set the scene first; the verb always waits at the end.

Rule 2 · how it works

Particles are tags — so word order is free

Each middle word wears a little tag (は wa, を o, に ni) that announces its job. Because the tag carries the role, you can shuffle the boxes and the meaning holds. English can't; Japanese and Hindi can.

Why it matters

This is the deepest difference from English. Once you trust the tags, you stop translating word-by-word and start reading by role.

Cue

Read the tag, not the position — wa = topic, o = object, wherever they sit.

は & です — the two bookends

In kore wa hon desu, only hon is a real word — は opens the topic, です closes the statement, and the noun rides between. Burn these two in first; they’re the frame, not vocabulary.

The core is smaller than you think (onion)

The opener is optional — Japanese drops the topic when it’s obvious: gakusei desu = “(I’m) a student”. So the irreducible core is just [Noun] + です; kore wa is scenery you wrap around it. And it’s the particle-lightest shape — mostly just は + です.

Is です actually used? (register)

Hugely — in polite speech (the safe default with anyone not close). Casual speech swaps it for plain だ or drops it (“kore, hon?”). Learn です first: high-frequency, and never rude.

The key that dissolves the confusion

Two classes, two origins. Treat a na-adjective as a NOUN (needs だ/です to state, な to touch a noun); an i-adjective is self-contained (ends -i, conjugates itself, attaches directly). That's the whole thing.

Watch a na-adjective mirror a plain noun move-for-move — only the linker differs (noun → の, na-adj → な):

Watch out — the trap

kirei (pretty) and kirai (disliked) end in -i but are actually na-adjectives (the -i is baked into the spelling, not the grammatical ending). You memorise this handful.

The practical rule

Default by the -i sound; learn the type with the word (like gender in Hindi). Beginner relief: [thing] wa [adj] desu works for BOTH — the split only bites when you (a) modify a noun (takai hon vs kirei na hon) or (b) conjugate plain/past/negative.

Hindi contrast

Hindi adjectives agree in gender/number (mehenga/mehengi). Japanese adjectives agree with nothing.
