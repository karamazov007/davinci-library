My Learnings · Craft & Systems

Development > Premise

The seed is handed to you. The growth is craft. Excellence isn’t in the starting point — it’s in everything you build on top, and in being pointedly great at a coherent few things rather than evenly good at all.

The premise is just the seed. The craft is everything that grows from it — and it’s almost always the real variable.
01

The Start Is Given · The Build Is Yours

People fixate on the seed because it’s visible and fixed. The people who win understood the growth was the real variable — the opposite of the “I need the perfect premise to start” trap.

02

Pointed Excellence Beats Even Adequacy

You don’t need every channel. Be outstanding on the ones your audience weights highest — a few loud knobs, coherently aimed — and you win the people who value them.

Adequate-at-everything is forgettable. That’s why Witch Hat bounced off — nothing turned up loud enough to be for someone specifically.
03

Two Edges — Where the Real Skill Lives

The thesis is true, but not unconditionally. These two edges keep it honest.

Edge 01 — Floors still exist

Craft isn’t free, and some seeds are salted ground

Some premises carry a structural flaw no development overcomes. And craft is the thing you spend years building — the person with a dull premise and no craft isn’t saved by this principle, they’re indicted by it.

Any start is good enough — for someone who did the work to earn the craft. Don’t let “the start doesn’t matter” become permission to skip the part that does.
Edge 02 — Coherence is required

Strong knobs one way · weak knobs kept quiet

JoJo’s weak realism doesn’t hurt — nothing was trying to be realistic, so the weak channel is silent, not fighting. But a weak channel that contradicts the strong one — gorgeous visuals on a confusing story, charisma undercut by visible dishonesty — is corrosive.

Be great at a coherent few, and keep the rest at least non-sabotaging.
A silent weakness is neutral. A weakness pulling against your strength is corrosive.
04

The Fully-Loaded Thesis

The version worth signing

Excellence is built in development, not handed to you in the premise — and it comes from being pointedly great at a coherent few things while keeping your weak points from actively working against you. Not from being evenly good at everything.

This Is Literally My Operating System

Enter a domain late, build the framework, automate through practice. I’ve run this for years — and I’m drawn to stories that dramatize the exact thesis I live by: a late, unpromising start plus relentless craft beats a gifted premise that never develops.

Proven across fitness, racket sports, and data science — the development, not the premise, decided it every time.

The Trap of Perfectionism

Perfectionism is the unreasonable, self-defeating ambition of getting something absolutely right — which makes us hard to be around and punishing to live within. The fault isn’t aiming high. It’s under-budgeting for how hard high actually is.

▶ Watch · School of Life: premature perfectionism ↗
01

It’s born in the imagination

The ease of picturing an ideal, set against the monstrous difficulty of building it. The sickness stagnates in that fertile gap.

02

The real fault: under-budgeting for difficulty

Our problems don’t arise from loving perfection — but from the reckless tendency to under-budget for what it costs. How well we budget depends on grasping the true, inherent difficulty of the task.

03

Premature perfectionism — the thing to criticise

High standards only turn toxic at one precise moment: when we expect something to be substantially easier than it is, and misread the struggle.

Same reality, two labels. The perfectionist picks the left — and pays for it.
04

Where it strikes — the under-budgeted timelines

Torture arrives when we imagine the summit is near, because we lack information on how hard others worked and how much they suffered to reach it.

Under-budgeted

A good novel in 6 months

The first draft is meant to be bad. Nobody told you.

Under-budgeted

A good career by 30

Measured against summaries, never against the years of grind behind them.

Under-budgeted

A good marriage, spontaneously

Worked out on the fly, with no model of how much it actually asks.

05

The cure: see the hidden labour

A better culture would endlessly show us the first drafts and hidden labours of others — and the true horrors exacted by anything worth doing.

The reframe worth keeping

The problem isn’t that we aim for perfection. It’s that we don’t have an accurate, redemptive idea of what perfection really demands.

Budget honestly for the difficulty → the weak beginning stops meaning anything about you. It’s difficult. You knew it would be.

06

The Perfectionist Trap

We aim at a career by admiring its masters — the airport architect, the star fund manager, the prize-winning novelist, the acclaimed chef. We form our plans on perfection. Then we take our first steps, and the trouble begins.

▶ Watch · School of Life: The Perfectionist Trap ↗
Definition

The perfectionist trap: a powerful attraction to perfection shorn of any mature understanding of what is actually required to attain it.

07

It isn’t your fault — the edited-out failure

The media quietly edits out billions of unremarkable lives, and the years of failure, rejection and frustration behind even the achievers — serving a curated reel of peak moments that then seems like the baseline, not the violent exception it really is.

We can’t forgive our early drafts — largely because we’ve never seen the early drafts of those we admire.
08

The price you can’t avoid

Allow yourself to do things quite imperfectly for a very long time — the toll for an opportunity one day, perhaps in decades, to do something others will call a “spontaneous success.”

09

The deeper root: it isn’t about the work

There’s a person we admire — up at dawn, rarely on holiday, always another hour of work, constantly promoted, never a late or sub-stellar piece. “High standards,” we say. But the concern isn’t their work — its recipients are privileged. It’s the state of their soul.

▶ Watch · School of Life: perfectionism & self-worth ↗
Perfectionism doesn’t spring from loving perfection. It springs from a deficit of early love.
10

The Sisyphean engine — ill, not driven

To expunge the feeling, we’ll do almost anything: work all hours, curry favour, do twice the next person. One part of the mind keeps promising the other that the next achievement will finally bring peace.

We’re ill, not driven. We don’t want perfect work — we’re escaping a feeling. Work is only the medium in which we strive to grow tolerable to ourselves.
Why work can’t fix it

The problem didn’t begin with work

So work can never be its solution. The real goal isn’t to be the ideal employee — it’s to feel acceptable.

Why you can’t outsource it

Bosses · customers · capitalism will never let you rest

By their nature — no evil intent — they always demand more. Acceptance handed to them is never granted.

11

The shift: acceptance from the start

We need to move our sense of where the drive comes from. Not an unnatural love of working perfectly — an unusually intense impression that we are dreadful people. Working harder cannot be the answer.

A welcome sign

Being able to tolerate a little laziness

Not because you’re idle — but because you’ve learnt to speak more kindly to yourself.

A fair anger

Aim it where it belongs

At those who couldn’t, at the outset, accept you for who you were — without a surfeit of trophies and prizes.

The line to keep

Working well is an admirable goal — but it becomes a symptom the moment it turns into cover for a secret aspiration to correct a deficit of early love.

You deserved acceptance from the start. It was never your job to earn the right to exist.

In one line

Aim as high as you like — but budget for the true cost, and read your early struggle as the price of a long road, not a verdict on your worth.
