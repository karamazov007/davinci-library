The reading toolkit

Lens 1

Fear, Respect & Usefulness

Almost every hold one person has over another is some mix of three currencies. Naming which one is in play — and how fragile it is — explains most of the power moves you’ll see.

😨 Fear — “I can hurt you.” Fast and reliable, but brittle. It breeds resentment, invites betrayal the moment it slips, and has to keep escalating. Rule by fear alone and you spend your life watching your back.
🫡 Respect — “I admire / believe in you.” Slow to build, durable, self-reinforcing. It buys loyalty that survives bad days — but it’s earned, easily spent, and one humiliating act can burn years of it.
🔧 Usefulness — “you need what I provide.” Transactional and conditional. Strong while you’re the supply — gone the instant someone cheaper, safer or closer appears. Being only useful makes you replaceable.
The real skill is the mix. The most secure figures combine all three — feared enough not to be crossed, respected enough to be followed, useful enough to be kept. Watch for who leans on just one — that’s exactly where they’re fragile.
Question to hold: for any relationship on screen — is this fear, respect, or usefulness doing the work? And what happens the day that one currency runs out?
Lens 2

Incentive analysis

“Show me the incentive and I’ll show you the outcome.” People rarely tell you what they want — but their situation does. Decode the incentive and the behaviour stops being surprising.

1 · Surface want. What they claim — the respectable, sayable version.
2 · Real want. What the situation rewards — money, status, safety, control, being seen.
3 · Constraint. What they can’t do — who they answer to, what they fear losing, the code they can’t be seen breaking.
4 · Likely move. Real want, routed around the constraint, gives you a prediction — often better than the character’s own.
Watch for conflicting incentives — the person who owes loyalty up and down the ladder, or whose money and safety pull opposite ways. That gap is where the double-crosses live.
Lens 3

Second-order thinking

First-order thinking asks “does this solve my problem now?” Second-order thinking asks “and then what?” Most tragedy here is a smart first-order move with a brutal second-order cost.

1st order — the immediate, intended result. As far as impulsive characters ever look.
2nd order — the reaction to the result. Who’s now angry, afraid, in debt to you, or gunning for you?
3rd order — the world you now live in. Trust eroded, a rival created, a rule broken that can’t be un-broken.
The pattern to spot: a move that’s +1 now, −5 later. When a character “wins” a scene, ask what that win will cost them by the finale.
Lens 4

Communication & influence

How does one person actually move another? The show is a masterclass in leverage that’s rarely stated out loud. These are the levers to name when you see them.

Leverage. Something they need or fear losing — money owed, a secret held, protection given. The quietest and strongest lever.
Reciprocity & favours. A gift or an “I did this for you” that creates a debt to be called in later.
The frame. Defining the situation first — “this is about respect,” “this is just business.” Whoever sets the frame usually wins the room.
Loyalty tests. Small asks that measure where someone really stands, long before the big ask comes.
Silence & omertà. What’s not said — the withheld approval, the pause, the code of not talking.
Displays. Generosity, rage, calm, a public reward or punishment — performed for the room as much as the target.
The tell: the most powerful influence is usually indirect — nobody says “do this or else.” When you feel the pressure in a scene but can’t point to a threat, name the lever. That’s the one to learn from.
Lens 5

Counterfactuals

“What if he’d done the other thing?” Counterfactuals test whether a choice actually mattered — and they’re some of the best conversations we’ll have.

Change ONE decision  →  trace the ripple forward  →  compare to what actually happened
Change one variable. Hold everything else fixed and flip a single choice. Change too much and it’s fan-fiction, not analysis.
Trace the ripple. Run it through the other lenses — whose incentives shift, whose fear/respect changes, what second-order costs appear.
Compare honestly. Was the real outcome inevitable given who these people are, or a genuine fork? Character as destiny vs. chance.
Why it’s worth it: counterfactuals separate the choices that mattered from the ones that only felt dramatic — and train you to see the forks in your own decisions before they close.
We’ll log the good ones here with both branches written out. Kept spoiler-safe — only on scenes you’ve already watched.
