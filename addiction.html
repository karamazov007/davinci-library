The Anatomy of a Hijacked Wanting System

Addiction is not a moral failure or a lack of willpower. It is a learning system doing exactly what it evolved to do — trained too well, on the wrong thing. Tab ① dismantles the machine — its parts, wiring and dynamics. Tab ② follows the deeper trap: how the mind's drive to automate turns boredom into the fuel and quietly hands itself over. The Core

One machine, described two ways

Strip away the substance or the behaviour and every addiction runs on the same underlying loop. Learn the loop once and you understand all of them. Two traditions — modern neuroscience and 2,500-year-old Buddhist psychology — mapped this exact machine from opposite ends and arrived at the same diagram.

An addiction is a prediction of relief or reward that has been learned so strongly it fires on its own cue — and then demands to be closed, whether or not it still delivers.

Notice what the loop implies. The craving is not the problem to be crushed; it is a signal — a learned prediction announcing that a reward is available. It carries no obligation. And the loop only tightens at step ⑤: every time the circuit is run to completion, the association it just used gets reinforced. So the leverage is not willpower applied at the moment of action — that is the loudest, most expensive point. The leverage is seeing the craving as a passing event and not feeding it, which lets the whole loop weaken on its own. Both traditions below are, at bottom, elaborate instructions for standing at that leverage point.

Lens 1 · Neuroscience

The wetware: a reward circuit, captured

The brain runs a reward-learning system whose job is to notice what predicts survival payoffs and to bias you toward repeating it. Addictive substances and behaviours are supernormal keys — they fit this lock harder than anything in nature — so they train the circuit far past its design tolerance. Here are the components and how they wire together.

Lens 1 · Neuroscience

Two dynamics that make it stick

Two experimental findings explain why cravings feel the way they do — and why they attach themselves to cues rather than to the reward itself.

1 · Dopamine is a teacher, not a treat

Record a dopamine neuron while an animal learns, and its behaviour is startling. It does not fire for pleasure — it fires for surprise about reward, and once a cue reliably predicts the reward, the signal jumps backward in time onto the cue. The craving you feel at a trigger is literally this signal firing: an old prediction, not a present need.

2 · Wanting outgrows liking

Because the circuit sensitizes with use, the motivational pull ("wanting") climbs while the actual pleasure ("liking") stalls or declines. The gap between the two curves is the felt experience of craving something that no longer satisfies.

Lens 1 · Neuroscience

The three-stage cycle — a spiral, not a circle

Zoom out from a single loop to the long arc of an addiction and Koob & Volkow describe three recurring stages, each in a different brain system. The key is that it is not a flat circle — each lap sinks a little lower, because the brain keeps re-setting "normal" downward.

Why the spiral tightens: opponent process & allostasis

Solomon & Corbit's opponent-process theory explains the sinking. Every strong pleasure (an a-process) automatically summons an opposing b-process that cancels it and lingers afterward. With repetition the b-process grows stronger and longer while the a-process shrinks. That is tolerance and withdrawal in one mechanism — and it drags the emotional baseline below zero.

Lens 2 · Buddhist First Principles

The dhamma: craving mapped from the inside

Twenty-five centuries before fMRI, Buddhist psychology mapped the same machine by careful first-person observation. Its central claim — the Second Noble Truth — is that the origin of suffering is taṇhā, craving: the mind's compulsive reaching for pleasant experience, away from unpleasant, and toward "more." Addiction is simply taṇhā that has found an especially reliable object.

"It is craving that leads to renewed becoming." The chain that turns a fleeting sensation into a compulsion is called paṭicca-samuppāda — dependent origination — and it has a seam you can pry open.
Lens 2 · Buddhist First Principles

The moving parts of craving

Six interlocking terms describe the machinery — and each maps cleanly onto something the neuroscientists later measured.

The two arrows — separating the event from the reaction

The Buddha's sharpest image for addiction's real cost. An urge (or a pain) arising is the first arrow — often unavoidable, a conditioned event. The second arrow is what we then do to ourselves: the grasping, the "I must," the story of lack. The first is a sensation; the second is the suffering — and it is optional.

Fire & fuel — why "just stop" misunderstands the flame

The Fire Sermon frames craving as fire. A fire is not a thing you can grab and remove; it is a process that continues only while fed. Clinging (upādāna, "fuel") is the feeding. You do not extinguish craving by fighting the flame — you starve it by no longer supplying fuel. The word nibbāna literally means "blowing out," as a flame goes out when its fuel is spent.

Synthesis

The whole system on one page

Everything above, assembled into a single machine: the spine of the loop, the Buddhist names on top, the neural mechanisms below, the two reinforcement loops that make it self-sustaining — and the single exit.

The design insight the whole model delivers: do not attack at Action. By the time craving has become action, the habit circuitry is already executing and the prefrontal brake is already weak — you are fighting on the worst possible ground. Every high-leverage move lives at the seam, between feeling and craving, where a small amount of clear awareness does what a large amount of willpower cannot.

Application

The way out — starve, don't fight

If the loop is fed by completion and dissolved by non-completion, then the whole strategy follows: meet the craving as a passing wave, decline to feed it, and let repetition run in reverse. This is not heroic resistance. It is patient un-learning.

The one principle under all of it: an addiction is a fed process, not a fixed thing. You do not need to overpower it — you cannot reliably out-muscle a system that weakens your muscle. You need to see it clearly enough that feeding it becomes optional, and then, wave by un-fed wave, let it starve. Understanding is not a preliminary to the work. Understanding, held as steady attention at the seam, is the work.

Part Two · The deeper mechanism

The Automation Trap

The same genius that makes you skilled — the mind's relentless drive to automate whatever it can predict — is the exact mechanism a superstimulus captures. This tab follows that capture from first principles: why the brain automates, how the craving creeps upstream until it seems to run on its own, why boredom becomes its fuel, and why "total takeover" is real as a feeling but false as a fact.

The fundamental problem is not that your mind is broken. It is that your mind is working perfectly — automating the predictable and hunting for surprise — inside an environment engineered to exploit exactly those two habits.
First principle

The mind automates everything it can

Deliberate thought is the brain's most expensive, slowest resource. So its default economics are simple: anything you can predict, offload to cheap automatic circuitry, and reserve costly attention for what is still surprising. Repetition is the conveyor belt that carries a behaviour from "decision" down into "reflex."

Your model

The craving creeps upstream

You spotted the real trajectory: at first the reward is a surprise, then the mind learns to predict it from the action, then it jumps the prediction back onto the cue — and it does not stop there. Through higher-order conditioning the trigger keeps creeping to ever-earlier links, so the craving fires sooner and sooner in the chain. This is temporal-difference credit assignment migrating to the earliest reliable predictor.

The key correction

The cue doesn't vanish — it moves inside

"A craving with no cue" is the trap's convincing illusion. What actually happens is that the trigger becomes internal and subtle — a mood, a flicker of fatigue, a transition between tasks, a time of day, a stray thought — so it drops below the waterline of awareness. The behaviour looks self-starting only because you can no longer see what starts it. And the deepest, most submerged cue of all is boredom itself.

There is a sliver of truth in the stronger claim, and it's worth keeping honest: sensitized circuits can fire semi-spontaneously, craving can incubate and grow during abstinence, and the mind's default-mode wandering throws up thoughts that then act as cues. So craving is not always reducible to a clean external trigger. But even the endogenous pull arises from a state — it is dependently arisen, not truly self-caused — which is exactly why it stays interruptible.

Mechanism

Why boredom and restlessness keep rising

Two separate engines drive it, and they compound. One is healthy and ancient; the other is manufactured by the modern reward environment.

Synthesis

The self-closing loop

Here your two observations fuse into one engine. Automation frees attention; the freed engine can't rest and goes seeking; that seeking is the restless neutral state; and that state has become the internal cue for the most over-learned reward path. Boredom is simultaneously the exit the addiction offers and the entrance it uses.

Buddhist bridge

The intolerable neutral

The dhamma names this with startling precision. Every contact carries a feeling-tone — pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral — and the untrained mind's specific weakness is that it cannot rest in the neutral. Neutral reads as "nothing," and the reflex is to convert it into something pleasant. Restlessness (uddhacca) is even listed as one of the five hindrances. Boredom, in this language, is simply the unbearable neutrality of an unstimulated moment.

Honest correction

"Takeover" is a shifted default, not a severed wire

You're right that it trends toward feeling like a hostile takeover — automaticity deepens, attention narrows onto the object, and the behaviour fuses with identity so it stops feeling like an intruder. But "completely out of the loop, running on its own" is not mechanically true, and believing it is the addiction's most useful lie. The aware override is never deleted. It just becomes the steeper, costlier path — no longer the default, but always still there.

The reframe

Mismatch, not malfunction

So the fundamental problem isn't your mind. It's the collision between machinery tuned for a world of scarce, effortful, delayed rewards and an environment engineered to deliver abundant, effortless, instant ones. That mismatch is the whole disease — and it points to two fronts, only one of which is the loop itself.

The cue to carry out of this tab: when the pull appears "from nowhere," don't fight it and don't obey it — ask what feeling you were just trying to leave. Nine times out of ten the honest answer is a flavour of restless, neutral under-stimulation. Name that, sit in it for one breath longer than is comfortable, and you've done two things at once: found the invisible cue, and started draining the reservoir. That is Lever 1 and Lever 2 in a single move.

Dependent origination

The urge doesn't arise alone — it's assembled

No single cause produces the urge. It ignites when several arrows arrive together: an internal push, an open window of dead time, easy opportunity, and a priming bodily context. Break enough of the arrows and the head can't form.

The search domino

One trigger deserves its own picture, because it isn't a single cause but a cascade: a harmless curiosity tips the first domino, and each step is a fresh novelty hit that green-lights a slightly stronger next one — higher-order conditioning happening live (D3·5/D3·6). By the time you notice, you're several dominoes down and the override is fighting downhill.
