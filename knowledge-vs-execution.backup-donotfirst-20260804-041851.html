Learning · First Principles

Knowing ≠ Doing

Your brain stores “knowing about” and “knowing how” in two separate machines — and there is no wire between them. You can’t pour theory in one and get skill out the other.

A theory and a competence aren’t one file in two formats — they’re two incompatible files in two incompatible systems.

Two memory systems, physically separate. One learns in a single exposure. The other only by repetition — and can’t be written to by words.

03

How Skill Is Actually Built

The conscious channel can only transmit a goal. A lower, parallel system builds the competence — one error-corrected, sleep-fed rep at a time.

Beginner clumsiness & exhaustion = the conscious system jammed into a loop it can’t run. The flood of errors is the learning.

External cues (a target in the world) free the wide parallel system. Internal cues (your own body parts) jam the narrow channel.

The single most useful practical lever — works in the pool, on the platform, in conversation, behind the camera.

07

In Practice

Five levers you can actually pull.

1Theory makes each rep informative

It can’t produce skill — wrong system. It sharpens the error signal and turns “something’s off” into a precise, correctable error.

2Watch for the switch-over point

Past a threshold, more reading just defers the reps. Switch when the next gain comes from contact with reality, not more pages.

3Protect sleep & spacing

Consolidation is where the wiring actually changes. Spaced practice with sleep beats the same volume crammed. Literal, not motivational.

4Use external focus everywhere

Aim attention at the goal in the world, not your body or performance — pool, platform, conversation, camera.

5Reframe the ugly early phase

Beginner clumsiness + exhaustion = one event: the conscious system drowning in errors. That flood is the learning.

The whole thing in one line

You can’t think your way into a skill — it lives in a system language can’t write to. Point the conscious mind at a clear goal, and let a slower, wider, sleep-fed system build the real machinery through error-corrected reps against reality.

08

The Method — A Cue-Library for a Narrow Channel

Since the declarative channel is too narrow to hold the skill, use it for what it is good at: a compact index of targeted fixes. Let the procedural system carry the execution. That division of labour is the method.

One pointer runs the motion. When a specific failure appears, swap in its phrase, apply it until it consolidates, then drop back. The channel holds one thing at a time.

The Pareto move is the real insight: don’t map the thousand things that could go wrong. Find the ~20% of failure modes wrecking 80% of the performance, and build cues only for those. A narrow channel’s scarce slots go to the highest-leverage corrections.

Most people never figure this out: the fix is not to widen the channel, but to spend its slots on the few failures that actually dominate.
09

Four Refinements — Where It Becomes Powerful or Quietly Fails

In order of how much they matter.

🗺️ Refinement 1 — The map is a hypothesis, not a blueprint (the big one)
You can’t know your real failure modes from the armchair — they depend on your proportions, mobility, history. Map lightly, make contact, then populate the library from observed errors, not predicted ones. (Your eyeglasses loop already worked this way.)
1️⃣ Refinement 2 — One conscious cue at a time
During correction the bandwidth ceiling still binds. Serial isn’t just tidier — parallel conscious correction re-jams the exact bottleneck the whole method exists to respect.
🌙 Refinement 3 — A workaround has to be trained out, not just applied
A fixed failure isn’t fixed the day you find the cue — it’s fixed after the cue has been drilled below conscious attention. It’s closed when you stop needing the phrase.
🎯 Refinement 4 — Prefer external, goal-shaped cues over internal, mechanism-shaped ones
Phrase your fixes as outcomes and external targets — even though the underlying problem is muscular. Same lever as §04, now applied to writing the cues.
10

The Method, Folded Together

The whole loop with the corrections built in — one pipeline, generalising across serve, stroke, chord transition, and the social and taste domains where the “failures” are recurring misreads.

A real methodology: map lightly, contact early, let reality surface the failures, build a small Pareto library of external-goal cues, correct strictly one at a time until each sinks below awareness, and keep the pointer on a system that increasingly runs itself.
The single thing to change

Stop trying to fully map and pre-solve before contact. The system you’re modelling is your own body — it only tells you its real failure modes once it’s moving.

11a

The Cue Loop

Zoom into the balancing loop. Every pass, your analysis must converge into a single cue — that cue is what you carry into the next session. Practice throws up new issues, those feed a fresh analysis, which yields the next cue. The loop never stops; it just keeps tightening toward ideal technique.

The same loop, one lap at a time: a cue goes into the next session → practice surfaces new issues → analysis in your notes (and with Claude) converges to one new cue → which goes back out. Round and round until the technique is as close to ideal as it gets.
↻Why the loop can't skip a stage

Analysis that never converges to a cue never reaches the cerebellum — it stays as words. And a session run without a cue generates errors you can't attribute. Each lap has to end in exactly one cue, so the next lap has something concrete to converge on.

11b

The Two Roles of a Cue

The same cue does two different jobs, and the shift between them is what “learning the skill” means — the declarative→procedural migration (Fitts & Posner's stages; Anderson's proceduralization). One phrase, but its referent migrates from an imagined target to a compiled program, and its ideal form compresses as it crosses over.

One cue throughout — its referent migrates from an imagined target to a compiled program, and its ideal form compresses from verbose/internal/decomposed to compressed/external/holistic as it crosses the associative middle.
1
🎯

Role 1 · the cue as target

Early on, the cue supplies the goal state — the reference your prediction-error engine compares against. “Push chest forward” hands the cerebellum a desired outcome, and rep by rep the forward model closes the gap. Its job is to specify the ideal.

the goal to close on
2
⚡

Role 2 · the cue as trigger

Once the movement is consolidated into a motor program, the cue no longer describes it — the description is baked into the engram. The same phrase stops being a blueprint and becomes an index that just fires it.

runs what you built
≈A gradient, not a switch

In the middle (associative) stage the cue does both jobs at once — partly still correcting, partly already retrieving. That's why a skill feels half-automatic for a while before it clicks. And the pointer never points at nothing: the referent is always something — early an imagined state, later a compiled program.

!Don't narrate a finished skill

Consciously narrating a compiled movement causes choking (Beilock's explicit-monitoring; Wulf's external-focus work). Once it's automatic, compress the cue — fewer words, external, holistic — and point at the effect, not the parts.

11c

Building the Cue — Two Phases

The two phases need genuinely different cue-building rules, because the cue is doing different jobs. Here's how to find and structure each.

🎯
Phase 1 · Learning

The teaching cue

Find it: diagnose the gap, isolate the vital few, and translate the single biggest error into the smallest concrete correction — “what one fix would fix the most?”

  • One fixworking memory is tiny — a cue that fixes two fixes neither
  • Concretea feel or checkable position — “heels together”, not “improve your kick”
  • Positivesay what to do — “chest up”, never “don't round your back”
  • Decomposemulti-part is fine here — you have the bandwidth and want the parts exposed
  • If–thenbind the correction to its trigger situation
→
⚡
Phase 2 · Mastery

The trigger cue

Find it: collapse the multi-part cue into the single word or image that evokes the whole movement — the “money moment” or the intended effect on the world. You're not describing; you're firing.

  • One wordone word or image, not a sentence — compression is the point
  • Externalpoint at the effect, not your body (Wulf) — and it guards against choking
  • Fire oncedon't re-narrate mid-move — that's exactly what causes choking (Beilock)
  • Feeloften a feeling or rhythm, not an instruction

↺Even non-motor cues carry their reasoning — then shed it

A learning cue can spell out its why (“the burn is safe — three more”) because it's still teaching your limbic system a reappraisal. Once that's baked in, the mastery cue drops the reasoning entirely — “more” now carries all of it.

The handoff rule: don't switch abruptly — as the skill consolidates, progressively compress and externalize the cue (shed a word here, trade a body-part for an outcome there) until one holistic pointer remains.
Which phase am I in?

If narrating the cue helps, you're still learning. The moment narrating it makes you clumsy, that skill has crossed over — time to compress to one word.

Two Regimes — Tight vs Loose Feedback

When the real feedback loop is loose, you compensate on two fronts — you import feedback from outside your own experience, and you manufacture sharper feedback inside it. Two different moves, solving two different problems, failing in two different ways.

Two moves for one problem: borrowing builds the map, manufacturing tests it. Keep them separate — they fail differently.
🌊
Tight feedback

Physics domains

  • ModelThin — barely needed
  • RepsHeavy · irreplaceable
  • CorrectsThe action, directly
  • Loopaction → world → corrected action
VS
🗣️
Loose feedback

Adaptive-agent domains

  • ModelHeavy · borrowed up front
  • RepsStill essential

  • Loopmodel → action → manufactured feedback → corrected model
In tight domains the loop closes on the action. In loose domains it closes on the model — the extra arc is the whole difference.
13

Move 1 · Borrow the Map

You're using other people's error signals because your own are too slow, noisy, or invisible. A biography lets you watch a mistake play out over years; an expert book is thousands of iterations someone else already paid for. Powerful — and the main way humans learn loose-feedback skills at all.

Borrowed feedback is brilliant for building the map. It is never proof the map is right.

Three distortions ride along with borrowed feedback — it's feedback you didn't generate, about situations that aren't yours, filtered through a narrator with an agenda.

1
🏆

Survivorship & narrative

Stories told after the fact by winners or people selling a framework. “I succeeded because X” is a reconstruction — a cleaned-up model of what worked, not the raw data.

often just wrong
2
🎬

Fictional feedback isn't real

Film sharpens the eye — you start seeing status moves and timing — but consequences are authored. A writer decided the line landed. Treat it as evidence and it installs false cause-and-effect.

eye yes, evidence no
3
💬

Reviews are the loosest

Reddit and social feedback are self-selected and performative — often the opposite of representative. Useful for surfacing which failure modes exist; near-useless for how common or consequential.

loosest of all
The crucial line

Borrowed feedback is for hypothesis-generation, not confirmation. Everything you read, watch, and gather is raw material for the map — none of it closes the loop. Which is exactly why Move 2 is non-negotiable.

14

Move 2 · Manufacture the Signal

Here's where validation actually happens. You artificially restore the properties that made water and piano good teachers — honest, fast, repeatable, failure-visible. Each tool is a targeted repair of one broken property.

Video restores honesty + visibility; a blunt observer restores an honest reaction; a fast debrief restores speed.
🎥
👁️

Video · the camera is your “water”

Stationary, non-adaptive, unflattering. It makes visible the failures you can't feel from inside — filler words, broken eye contact, a rushed pace — converting a self-masked signal into an honest external one.

🗣️
🎯

Blunt observer · licence honesty

Recruit one person and explicitly license them to be undiplomatic. The instruction has to be specific or politeness leaks back in.

“tell me the moment I lost you”, not “how was I?”
⏱️
🔎

Debrief · hunt the invisible

Review the interaction against your model while memory is fresh, specifically hunting the failures no ambient signal flagged. You are manually shortening a loop reality left long.

The deep point

Manufactured feedback is often more real than the “real” loop. Ambient feedback is confounded, delayed, and polite; your read of “how did that go?” is frequently an illusion. The manufactured loop actually correlates with the truth — so it isn't a poor substitute, it's often the superior signal, full stop.

15

The Integrated Loop

Does the old “do, then do, then do” still hold? Yes — the reps never leave. What changes is that in a loose domain you wrap them in two scaffolds the physics domains don't need: a borrowed model going in, and a manufactured loop coming out.

Four steps. Step 4 is the one that distinguishes the regime: the feedback corrects your model, not only your action.
AThe reps never leave

The procedural skill — real-time reading, timed production — can only be grown by execution, exactly as in swimming. Reading never builds the motor half. The model aims the reps; it doesn't replace them.

BStep 4 is the whole difference

In swimming the water corrects your action directly and you barely need a model. Here, the manufactured feedback corrects your map — you discover which borrowed rules were false and revise the model.

16

Two Dangers to Hold

This method has failure modes of its own. Both are quiet — they feel like progress while they go wrong.

⚠️ Danger 1 — the model can eat the reps
The borrowed-feedback phase generates no failure and no exposure — which is exactly why it's dangerous. Cap it; force the switch to contact.
⚠️ Danger 2 — borrowed rules masquerade as validated
Loose feedback won't cleanly falsify a wrong model, so a rule can feel confirmed for years. Keep every borrowed rule tagged unvalidated until your own manufactured feedback has tested it.
The whole thing in one line

In loose-feedback domains you compensate for a bad natural teacher by borrowing others' feedback to build the map and manufacturing your own to test it — but the reps stay central, the borrowed model is only ever a hypothesis, and the manufactured loop is what keeps that hypothesis honest.

Blitz Acquisition

How to borrow someone else's method and leap — fast. Use it when you don't yet fully understand a skill, or when you've hit a ceiling: instead of grinding alone, copy a high-quality performer's movement directly and let your nervous system converge on it.

When to blitz

1 · You lack the understanding. You don't know the first principles yet, and building them from scratch is slow. Borrow a working template now; understand it later.

2 · You've hit a ceiling. Your own trial-and-error has plateaued. Someone better already encodes the answer in their body — copy it to break through.

The core routine
The Chameleon Method

The cycle

Decompose → Observe → Imitate → Perform → Repeat

The whole method is a short loop. Break the skill into parts, watch a good player a piece at a time, copy the movement, then perform without micromanaging — and repeat until it converges.

Step 1 · Decompose

Name the components first

Before you watch anyone, know what to look at. Any physical game breaks into a handful of nameable components — in bowling: stance & feet, grip, arm swing, timing, release point, follow-through, balance. Components turn a blur into a checklist.

Step 2 · Observe

One or two components per watch — never everything at once

Watch a strong player across several attempts, but each pass focus on only one or two components. Trying to absorb the whole motion at once gives you nothing; isolating parts gives your brain a clean template for each.

Step 3 · Imitate

Become the chameleon

Copy the movement as closely as you can — even if it feels awkward. Don't ask whether it feels natural; that question comes later. Temporarily borrow their body.

Step 4 · Perform & converge

Stop steering; let it emerge

Now do the shot without consciously controlling each part. Let the nervous system integrate what it observed, and the movement emerges as one motion.

Why it works

A great performer is a higher-fidelity template than words

Verbal instruction and trial-and-error hand your body a noisy, low-fidelity template — lots of detours before it locks in. Copying a high-quality performer hands it a high-fidelity visual template directly, so convergence is far faster. That's the whole edge of blitzing.

The precondition — without it, the method collapses
The nerve to switch

The Chameleon Method only fires if you have the courage to drop your own method mid-game and copy someone whose is working. That takes being impersonal to the result — your worth is not on the table. Every new piece of information is there to teach you, not to rate you. Lose that nerve and you get self-conscious, and self-consciousness kills the final step: perform, and stop controlling.

The trigger

When your own model quietly breaks

Today: a heavier ball, thin and uneven oil, and you couldn't read the middle arrow — so the line you've grooved simply didn't work. That's the exact moment the method is for.

Read it correctly

A bad result confirms exactly one thing

The ball missing the pocket proves only that the causes you're applying aren't producing the effect you want, here. It is not a statement about your worth. Treating it as data — not a verdict — is what keeps you calm enough to adapt.

The first-principles move

Cancel what's shared — the difference is the method

The conditions are roughly the same for everyone on that lane. Subtract those shared causes, set natural ability aside, and the only visible difference left is method. If yours has failed four or five times, repeating it won't carry you across — watching someone whose method works, and copying it, will.

The fork

Impersonal vs. self-conscious

The same bad result can go two ways. Take it personally and you freeze; treat it as data and you switch, then perform freely. The nerve decides which branch you take.

Why nerve is load-bearing

Courage keeps the last step open

Imitating gets you a template, but the payoff is in letting go to perform. Fear clamps precisely the thing that step needs relaxed — so without courage, the whole method stalls at the finish.

The essence

When you're short on understanding or stuck at a ceiling, don't reinvent — copy. Decompose the skill, observe a great player one part at a time, imitate exactly, then let go and let it converge. Borrowed motion first; understanding follows.
