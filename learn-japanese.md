# PART A · Meta — How to learn ANY language

*The reusable recipe. This is the real long-term prize.*

## The phase model

A rough arc for taking on any new language. Refine as we test it against Japanese.

1. **Month 1 — crack the meta-structure.** Sentence structure, word modification, and the handful
   of core principles below, plus the caveats/intricacies you keep tripping on. Don't chase
   vocabulary yet; chase the *skeleton*.
2. **Word accumulation.** Once the skeleton holds, it's a numbers game — convert words relentlessly
   (Google Translate), slot them into structures you already own. Fluency ≈ words × structure.
3. **Conversation.** Roleplay / "playwriting" — script and act out real-ish conversations to
   pressure-test structure + words under something like live conditions.
4. **Writing (the script).** Last. Learn to *write* the language — especially non-Latin scripts —
   and the meta-structure for how writing systems work.

## The 4–5 principles to attack first

*The month-one checklist for any language. Draft — sharpen as Japanese teaches us what really matters.*

1. **Word order** — SVO? SOV? VSO? (English SVO · Hindi SOV · Japanese SOV.)
2. **Role marking** — how does the language show who-did-what-to-whom? Word position, particles,
   postpositions, case endings?
3. **Word modification** — tense, aspect, politeness, number, comparison: what changes on the word?
4. **Fusion / agglutination** — when do separate words merge into one (or take glued-on endings)?
5. **Caveats & intricacies** — the high-frequency exceptions that break the clean rules.

## Generalization log

*When a Japanese finding turns out to be a general principle, record it here so it transfers.*

- **Position-based vs marker-based role assignment.** English assigns roles by word *position*;
  Japanese (particles) and Hindi (postpositions) assign them by *markers*. First question to ask of
  any new language: which one is it?
- **Questions: particle vs inversion.** Japanese *ka* (end) and Hindi *kya* (front) form questions
  with a dedicated particle; English inverts word order. Axis: "how does this language mark a question?"
- **Wh-words: in-situ vs fronting.** Japanese and Hindi leave the question word where the answer
  goes; English moves it to the front. Axis: "does the wh-word move?"
- **Predicate-final.** Japanese and Hindi both put the predicate last. Axis: word order (SOV/SVO/…).

## B1 · Sentence structure

**Cue:** *Every Japanese sentence is one of **three shapes**, decided by its **last word** (the
predicate): a **noun + desu**, an **adjective + desu**, or a **verb**. Learn the three tails and
you can build anything.*

### The two rules that never break
1. **The predicate goes last.** The final word is always a **verb**, an **i-adjective**, or the
   **copula です/だ (the "is")** — everything else is setup in front. (Hindi shares this; English
   drops the verb/"is" into the middle.)
2. **Particles mark roles, so word order is free.** Each middle word is tagged by a particle
   (は wa, を o, に ni…) that states its job. The *tag* carries the meaning, so the middle slots can
   be shuffled. English uses **position** to assign roles ("dog bites man"); Japanese uses
   **markers** (and Hindi uses postpositions). Deepest difference from English.

### Clarification — can a NOUN be the predicate? (yes — via the copula)
Satyam's question: if the verb/adjective/"is" always ends the sentence, can a noun never be the
predicate / never come last?

**Answer: nouns absolutely can be predicates — that's Skeleton 1.** But a noun predicate is not a
*bare* noun; it's **noun + です** (copula; plain だ, polite です). The noun supplies the meaning
("a book"), です turns it into a statement ("*is* a book"). So the noun comes at the end of the
*content*, and です — the true final token — clings right after it.

- Where the "is" sits: **JP** *kore wa **hon desu*** (कोरे वा होन देस) · **HI** *ye **kitaab hai*** ·
  **EN** *this **is** a book* — JP/HI park the copula at the very end (after the noun); English puts
  it in the middle.
- So the final token of any sentence is exactly one of: a **verb** (tabemasu), an **i-adjective**
  (takai), or the **copula です/だ** (which closes every **noun** predicate *and* every **na-adjective**
  predicate). Verbs and i-adjectives stand alone; nouns and na-adjectives lean on です to reach the end.
- Nuance (later): in casual speech です/だ can be dropped (*kore, hon?* / *gakusei!*), and then the
  noun really is the last word. Standard/polite form keeps です.
- **Cue:** for an "A is B" sentence, don't stop at the noun — cap it with **です**; the "is" always lands last.

### Skeleton 1 — Noun predicate (A = B) → identity, naming, "this is a ___"
Frame: `[Topic] wa [Noun] desu.`
- This is a book. → *ye ek kitaab hai* → **kore wa hon desu.**
- My name is Satyam. → *mera naam Satyam hai* → **watashi no namae wa Satyam desu.**
- Demonstratives: **kore** (this) · **sore** (that, by you) · **are** (that, over there) · **dore** (which).
- Covers: "my name is…", "that is a phone / power bank / laptop."

**Anatomy (Satyam's model, refined) — think onion, core-out:**
- **です** = the anchor, always last, does the "is" (flexes: deshita/ja arimasen, but stays at the end).
- **the noun** = the meat; the swap slot (hon, pen, megane, sumaho…).
- **[opener + は]** = *optional* theme (demonstrative kore/sore/are, or any noun + は).
- **Irreducible core = [Noun] + です.** The opener is scenery — Japanese drops the topic when obvious
  (*gakusei desu* = "(I'm) a student", no opener). So the *predicate* = Noun + です; the topic wraps around it.
- **Particle-light shape.** Only real tag is は on the opener (+です at the end). Particles bloom in the
  *verb* predicate; inside the noun slot the one that shows up is **の** for bigger nouns (*Ram no hon*).
- Same shape as Hindi: *yeh kitaab hai* = opener → noun → copula-at-end; JP just swaps *hai* → *desu*.

**は and です — the two bookends (the highest-leverage pieces).** In *kore **wa** hon **desu***, は and です
are the *frame* and *hon* is the only content word. は opens (topic), です closes (statement). Burn these
two in first — they're skeleton, not vocabulary; you just swap the middle.
- **は (ha, said *wa* · वा) = topic marker** — "as for X…". Backbone of JP info order: topic first, comment
  after. Marks *topic*, not strictly grammatical subject (→ the は-vs-が puzzle; the one place Hindi won't help).
- **です = polite copula ("is")** — finishes the sentence (predicate-last anchor) *and* marks politeness.

**Is です actually used? (register).** Yes — hugely — in **polite** speech (です・ます style), the safe
default with anyone not close: strangers, colleagues, shops, teachers, public. Casual speech swaps it for
plain **だ (da)** or drops the copula (*"kore, hon?"*, *"gakusei"*). For a learner: **learn です first, use
it freely** — high-frequency, never rude; worst case slightly formal among friends. だ comes later.

**ko-so-a-do — the full grid (one pattern, three uses).** Every pointing word = **prefix + stem**.
Prefix = distance/which: **こ ko-** near me · **そ so-** near you · **あ a-** far from both · **ど do-** question.
Stem = kind: **-re** = a thing · **-no** = + a noun · **-ko** = a place.

| | thing (-re) | + noun (-no) | place (-ko) |
|---|---|---|---|
| こ near me (this/here) | kore | kono | koko |
| そ near you (that/there) | sore | sono | soko |
| あ far (over there) | are | ano | asoko |
| ど question (which/where) | dore | dono | doko |

- 4 prefixes × 3 stems = 12 words from 7 pieces. Examples: *kore wa hon desu* (this is a book) · *kono hon*
  (this book) · *toire wa **doko** desu ka* (where is the toilet?).
- **New concept:** JP has **three** distances; English (this/that) and Hindi (*yeh/voh*) have only **two**.
  The extra one is **そ so- = near the *listener***. That "near you" zone is the only genuinely new idea here.
- (More stems exist later: こちら/そちら (direction, polite) · こんな (this kind of) · こう/そう/どう (this way/how).)

### Skeleton 2 — Verb predicate (A does B) → actions, motion
Frame: `[Topic] wa [Object] o [Verb].`  (+ optional slots: destination *ni/e*, place-of-action *de*, etc.)
- I eat sushi. → *main sushi khaata hoon* → **watashi wa sushi o tabemasu.**
- I go to school. → *main school jaata hoon* → **watashi wa gakkou ni ikimasu.**
- Covers: "I'm eating", "I'm doing", "I'm going to [place]."

### Skeleton 3 — Adjective predicate (A is [quality]) → descriptions
Frame: `[Topic] wa [Adjective] desu.`
- This is expensive. → *ye mehenga hai* → **kore wa takai desu.**
- Japanese has two adjective classes (*i-* and *na-*) that modify differently — flagged here,
  detailed in B3. It's a change to the *tail*, not a new shape.

**i-adjective vs na-adjective — the key that dissolves the confusion.** Two classes because two
origins. **Trick: treat a *na*-adjective as a NOUN, and an *i*-adjective as a self-contained
(verb-like) word.**
- **i-adjective** ends in **-i** (takai, oishii, ookii, omoshiroi). Self-sufficient: carries its own
  tense/negation (*takai* is, *takakatta* was, *takakunai* isn't) and glues **straight** onto a noun
  (*takai hon*). です with it = politeness only (never *takai da*).
- **na-adjective** behaves like a **noun**: needs copula **だ/です** to predicate (*kirei da/desu*) and
  **な** to touch a noun (*kirei na hana*). Tense via the copula (*kirei datta*, *kirei ja nai*).
- **The parallel (the whole thing):** na-adj mirrors a plain noun move-for-move. `hon desu / nihongo NO
  hon / hon datta` ≡ `kirei desu / kirei NA hana / kirei datta`. Only the linker differs: nouns use
  **の**, na-adjectives use **な**. (That's why they're *na*-adjectives.) → Satyam's instinct that "they
  categorise nouns" was right: な is doing の's linking job.
- **"Always ends in -i?"** No — only i-adjectives. **Trap:** *kirei* (pretty), *kirai* (disliked) end
  in -i but are **na**-adjectives (the -i is in the spelling, not the grammatical ending).
- **Rule:** default by the -i sound; memorise the handful of -i exceptions; otherwise **learn the type
  with the word** (like gender in Hindi). Beginner relief: *X wa [adj] desu* works for BOTH — the split
  only bites when (a) modifying a noun (*takai hon* vs *kirei na hon*) and (b) plain/past/negative
  conjugation.
- *(Future viz: a 3-row comparison — noun / na-adj / i-adj × predicate / modify / past — showing na-adj
  ≡ noun. Skeleton 3 modal is thin; good place to build it.)*

### The question transform (rides on top of any skeleton)
- **Yes/no:** add **ka** at the **end** → **kore wa hon desu ka.** (Is this a book?)
  Hindi adds **kya** at the **front**; both use a question *particle*, where English flips word order.
- **Wh-question:** swap the unknown for a question word + **ka**; the word **stays in place** (no
  fronting like English). Words: **nani** (what) · **doko** (where) · **dare** (who) · **itsu** (when)
  · **dou** (how) · **naze/doushite** (why).
  - What are you eating? → **nani o tabemasu ka.**
  - Where are you going? → **doko e ikimasu ka.**
  - Where are you? → **anata wa doko desu ka.** (Skeleton 1 with *doko* in the noun slot.)

### Payoff — every example collapses into the categories
| English | Category | Skeleton |
|---|---|---|
| I'm eating / I'm doing | action | S2 verb |
| I'm going to [place] | motion | S2 verb (+ *ni/e*) |
| My name is X | identity | S1 noun + *desu* |
| This/that is a book/phone/laptop | identification | S1 noun + *desu* |
| What are you eating? | action + wh | S2 + *ka* |
| Where are you going? | motion + wh | S2 + *ka* |
| Where are you? | location | S1 + *ka* |

→ Eight different English sentences reduce to **three skeletons + one transform.**

## B2 · Particles (the workhorses)

*The little markers that do the grammar. This is where most of the structure lives.*

| Particle | Rough job | Hindi analogue (loose) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| は (wa) | topic marker ("as for…") | — (Hindi has no exact topic marker) | to fill |
| が (ga) | subject marker | ने (ne), sometimes | to fill |
| を (o) | direct object | को (ko) / unmarked | to fill |
| に (ni) | to / at / time / indirect obj | को / में / पर | to fill |
| で (de) | at / by means of | में / से | to fill |
| の (no) | possessive / linking | का/की/के | to fill |
| から / まで | from / until | से / तक | to fill |

*(All entries provisional — verify and expand as we go.)*

### The bridge — Hindi postpositions ARE Japanese particles
Satyam's realization (and it's the biggest head start in the language): Hindi's का/के/की, में, को, से,
पर are the **same machine** as Japanese particles — a tag that clips onto a word, marks its role, and
**travels with it** as a unit, which is exactly why word order flexes. की = Ram's tag; の is its twin.

Worked parallels:
- *This is Ram's book* → HI *yeh Ram **kī** pustak hai* → JP *kore wa Ram **no** hon desu* (राम नो होन देस)
- *This book is Ram's* → HI *yeh pustak Ram **kī** hai* → JP *kono hon wa Ram **no** desu* (の can stand alone)
- *This book's cover is red* → HI *is pustak **kā** cover lāl hai* → JP *kono hon **no** hyōshi wa akai desu*
  — note *is pustak kā cover* maps unit-for-unit onto *kono hon no hyōshi*: possessor + tag + possessed.

Rosetta (rough): का/के/की → **の** · में → **に**(exist)/**で**(action) · को → **を**/**に** ·
से → **から**/**で**/**と** · पर → **に**/**で** · ने → **が**(loose) · तक → **まで**.

**Three caveats so we don't over-trust it:**
1. **Not 1-to-1.** One Hindi postposition often fans into several JP particles (में→に/で, को→を/に,
   से→から/で/と). Can't blind-swap; the senses are carved up differently — that's where we slow down.
2. **का agrees, の doesn't.** की/का/के change with gender/number; の has one form — JP is *easier* here.
3. **The one gap:** Japanese は (topic) has no clean Hindi equivalent. So は vs が is the one place the
   native instinct goes quiet — expect friction there.

## B3 · Word modification

*How words change: tense, politeness, negation, and fusion.*

- **Agglutination:** Japanese verbs build meaning by gluing endings onto a stem —
  e.g. 食べ (tabe-, "eat") → 食べます (tabemasu, polite) → 食べました (tabemashita, past) →
  食べたい (tabetai, "want to eat") → 食べない (tabenai, negative). One word carries what English
  needs several for.
- **"Two words become one":** e.g. te-form + auxiliary → 食べている (tabete iru, "is eating").
- _Contrast to watch:_ Hindi tends to use **separate auxiliary words** (खा रहा हूँ = "eat -ing am"),
  where Japanese **fuses** into one. Same job, different packaging — a good generalization candidate.

## B4 · EN → HI → JP workbench

*The core method. Each example: English, then Hindi, then Japanese, then what the mapping teaches.*

**Format example (illustrative — not from a session yet):**

- **EN:** I eat an apple. — *SVO*
- **HI:** मैं सेब खाता हूँ। — *main seb khaataa hoon* — "I apple eat-am" — *SOV*
- **JP:** 私はりんごを食べます。 — *watashi wa ringo o tabemasu* — "I(topic) apple(obj) eat" — *SOV*
- **Mapping / lesson:**
  - Both HI and JP put the **verb last**; English doesn't. English→Hindi is where the reorder happens.
  - JP marks roles with particles (は topic, を object); Hindi here leaves सेब unmarked (को is used
    for specific/animate objects). → *role-marking differs; note for the Particles tab.*
  - JP **fuses** the verb (tabe+masu = one word); Hindi **splits** it (khaata + hoon). → *fusion vs
    auxiliary — candidate for the Generalization log.*

### 2026-08-09 — Discussion: i-adjective vs na-adjective (demystified)
**Takeaway:** Two adjective classes because two origins. **Key: na-adjective = behaves like a NOUN (needs だ/です to predicate, な to touch a noun); i-adjective = self-contained/verb-like (ends -i, conjugates itself, attaches directly).** na-adj mirrors a plain noun move-for-move; only the linker differs (noun→の, na-adj→な). ### 2026-08-09 — Discussion + build: the particle RULE, and a frequency treemap
**Takeaway (the rule):** Particles attach to **roles, not words**. Don't ask "what particle does this word take?" — ask "what **job** is it doing in the action?"; the job picks the particle. Same noun flips particles as its job changes: *gakkou **ni** iku* (to school) · *gakkou **de** benkyou* (study at) · *gakkou **o** deru* (leave) · *gakkou **wa**…* (topic). Two layers: (1) role-based, ~80% predictable; (2) a few **verb-governed** quirks to memorise as verb+particle units (*tomodachi ni au* = meet · *basu ni noru* = ride · *sushi ga suki* = like). In-the-moment method = a checklist (doer? done-to? where-to? where-at? with? from? when?) → the answer names the particle (that checklist is a future **flowchart**).

Role → particle: doer/subject **が**(/は) · object **を** · destination/recipient/time **に** · place-of-action/means **で** · toward **へ** · with/and **と** · from/until **から/まで** · of **の**. (Same instinct as Hindi: *school ko jaana* vs *sushi khaana*.)

**Built:** a **frequency treemap** in the Particles tab (per the viz playbook — area = frequency, learn biggest first). ### 2026-08-09 — Discussion: は & です (the bookends), ko-so-a-do grid, です register
