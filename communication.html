Human Dynamics

Everything I've worked out about dealing with people, as one connected stack: charisma gets you taken seriously → the lifecycle + actions are the vehicle that builds → relationship first-principles are the trust that accumulates → influence are the levers you cash on that trust. Trust multiplies every lever.

① Charisma — the gate② Relationship principles — the trust③ Lifecycle + actions — the vehicle④ Influence — the levers
The stack
Four layers, one flow — each enables the next

Read it bottom to top. Charisma is the gate — it decides if you're even taken seriously. The lifecycle + your actions are the process that builds the relationship first-principles — the trust that accumulates as a state. And influence sits on top: the levers you pull on a specific decision, whose power is multiplied by the trust underneath. Skip a layer and the one above it wobbles.

① Charisma
Presence · Power · Warmth — the triad

From The Charisma Myth: charisma is a set of learnable behaviours, not a gift. Its base is the two universal social axes — Power (perceived ability to affect the world) and Warmth (goodwill) — and Presence (undivided attention) is the amplifier that makes both land. Presence is also the bridge to the layer above: attention makes people feel seen, which is the start of trust.

② Relationship first-principles
The drivers — read as a radar (area = closeness)

The needs a bond must meet. Bigger enclosed area = closer relationship — with a catch: Safety is a gate, not just area (near-zero safety nulls the whole thing, so it multiplies rather than adds). Responsiveness — feeling understood, valued, cared for — is the core that absorbs "validation." And Reciprocity flips: score-kept early, it converts to communal (give by need, stop counting) at the top. Warmth × competence are the readouts these produce.

③ The vehicle · part 1
The Communications Lifecycle — a timeline

The lifecycle is the time-sequenced medium that delivers the drivers stage by stage — it's not separate, it's the process the state is built through. Notice the tags: each stage feeds specific relationship first-principles, and Active Listening (your presence) runs beneath all seven — it's how anything actually lands.

③ The vehicle · part 2
Actions → the lever each one moves

Your question — "is there a first principle for actions?" — has a clean answer: no separate one; actions are derived from the relationship drivers. You don't memorise a list of moves — you pick a lever and ask "what raises it?" Communication alone won't get you there; the actions (favours, reliability, inclusion, shared experiences) are what physically move the levers the talk can only point at.

④ Influence
Four families of levers, on a base of trust

The levers you pull on a decision, sorted into four families — all standing on trust, which multiplies them. Two corrections to the classic list: you rightly moved Liking down to the foundation (it's the substrate, not a trick), and the honest additions are commitment/consistency, reciprocity, and reason/framing — the last being the common blind spot (people are also moved by a good case, well-framed).

Why trust sits under it all
The multiplier — same lever, different outcome

This is why the layers are ordered the way they are. The identical influence move — an authority cue, a scarcity frame — lands as leadership from someone trusted and bounces as manipulation from someone who skipped the trust-building. Trust isn't a nice-to-have on top; it's the multiplier that decides whether any lever works at all.

The line that keeps it clean
Which direction you point the levers

The whole stack is ethically neutral machinery — the same levers build or manipulate depending on where you aim them. Pointed at the other person's genuine interest, on a base of real trust, it's just good leadership and it compounds. Pointed against it, it's manipulation that spikes then collapses — the recurring line across the whole library.

The one cue — Build bottom-up: be present (charisma) → run the lifecycle and do real favours (vehicle) → so the relationship drivers accumulate (trust) → then, and only then, pull an influence lever, aimed at their genuine interest. Skip the base and the top is just manipulation that won't hold.

The takeaway

One system, four layers: charisma opens the door, the lifecycle + actions build the relationship first-principles (trust), and influence is what you cash on that trust — multiplied by it. Everything else in this tab is a zoom-in on one of these layers. Master them in order, aim them toward people's real interests, and you can move almost anything without breaking anything.

The graceful no

Excuses Creation

The craft of stepping out of something without damaging the relationship — a believable, kind, low-friction reason that lets both sides save face. Not about elaborate lies; about a clean, respected exit.

The anatomy
What makes an excuse land cleanly

A good excuse is believable because it fits you, specific enough to feel real but not so detailed it invites questions, hard to falsify, and easy for the other person to accept without losing face. The green one matters most: offer a soft alternative ("can't this week — next Tuesday?") so the no feels like a redirect, not a rejection.

The manner
How to deliver it

The delivery matters more than the content. Brevity signals truth — the more you justify, the less believable you sound. Say it early (last-minute bails cost trust), keep the story consistent, and keep the warmth so the relationship survives the no.

Cue — Short, early, calm, and with a soft alternative. If you feel the urge to add a third reason, stop — you've already said enough.

The takeaway

The goal isn't a clever lie — it's a respected exit. A believable, brief, kind reason that fits you and offers a redirect lets you step out of things while keeping the relationship intact. That's the whole skill.

Playful, low-stakes talk

Bakaiti

The art of light, playful, no-agenda banter — the goofing-around, riffing, gentle teasing that makes people relax, laugh, and warm to you. It looks like "nothing," but it's how bonds actually form.

What it does
Why bakaiti is a real skill, not just timepass

Bakaiti is how you signal "I'm safe, warm and fun to be around" without saying it. It lowers everyone's guard, builds closeness quickly, and lightens tense moments. It's the exact fuel for climbing rungs 3–4 of the Closeness Pyramid — shared jokes and easy rapport.

How to do it
Moves that make banter land — and lines not to cross

The engine is "yes-and": take whatever's in front of you and riff — exaggerate it, call back to an earlier joke, tease yourself first. Read the room so you're adding lightness, not noise, and always punch up or at yourself, never down. Warm-and-playful builds bonds; sharp-and-clever at someone's expense breaks them.

Cue — Yes-and, callback, tease yourself first. Keep it kind, read the room, and quit while it's still fun.

The takeaway

Bakaiti isn't wasted time — it's the social glue. Light, kind, playful riffing is how you make people comfortable and how rapport actually gets built. Master it and you become the person others relax around and want near.

Before you spend energy, place the person. Two axes: how connected they are (reach) and how much trust the tie can carry. A hub earns the reach track; a trusted ally (your grapevine insider) earns the deep track; someone you simply meet a lot you just keep warm at the base; and the rare person high on both gets the full investment.

The two targets need different things — a hub gives you reach, an ally gives you reliability — but you build both with the same tools: the Comms Lifecycle to get in, the Ladder of Closeness to go deep. You just climb higher with the ally and stay broader with the hub.

Depth is one ladder for everyone: open at the base with anyone (the plan, the food, "which team?"), step up to work & company for shared ground, to interests where the spark lives, and to the personal top only with your handful. Climb a rung only when you feel a spark — most ties stay near the base, and that's correct.

Cue — Assess cold, connect warm. Place the person (hub / ally / keep-warm), run the Lifecycle to get in, climb the Ladder as the spark allows, and bank favours you'll maintain. The takeaway

This is one system, not five notes. For anyone worth it: work out whether they're a hub (reach) or a trusted ally (reliability), use the Comms Lifecycle to get in, climb the Ladder of Closeness as far as the spark allows, and build a Favor Bank you keep alive. Same machinery, tuned to the node — and the way to make it real is reps: quizzes, case questions, and roleplay.

How you carry yourself

Personality Building

The craft of conducting oneself — when to speak and when not to, how to stay composed, how to negotiate, how to think, and how to present yourself — plus a first-principles model of what personality even is, so you can honestly assess yourself and others and tell growth from stagnation. Not a fixed trait you're born with, but a skill you build: read it, watch it, extract the move, and practise it live.

The map
The dimensions of conducting yourself

"Personality" isn't one thing — it's a handful of trainable dimensions: how you carry yourself, how you use speech, how steady you stay, how you handle conflict, how clearly you think, and how you show up. Name them, and each becomes something you can practise instead of something you just "are."

First principles
What personality is made of — core to surface

From first principles (a usable model, not a clinical one), personality stacks from a deep, given core to a trainable surface: your temperament (wiring you're mostly born with) → your values & drives (what you want) → your traits & habits (how you tend to act) → your skills (what you can actually do) → your presentation (how it all shows up). The deeper the layer, the slower it moves — so aim most of your effort at the outer layers, where change is fast, and let the pressure slowly reshape the inner ones.

Speech
When to speak — and when silence is the stronger move

Most people speak to fill space or relieve their own tension. Conducting yourself well means running a quick filter first — true? useful? the right moment? — and if not, holding. Silence isn't absence; it's a move, and often the stronger one. Say less, mean it, and let the quiet carry weight.

Composure
Use the gap — respond, don't react

Between the thing that provokes you and your reaction, there's a gap — a single breath. Reactive people collapse it and act on impulse; composed people use it and choose. Carrying yourself well is mostly this: steady voice, unhurried, warm — because you're responding from the gap, not firing from the trigger.

Negotiation
Dig beneath positions — trade, don't fight

Positions clash; interests often don't. What each side says it wants is the surface — dig to what they actually need, and you usually find room to trade something cheap for you that's valuable to them. Know your walk-away (BATNA) so you're never trapped, expand the pie before splitting it, and win the deal without wounding the person.

The self-check
A radar to assess yourself — and others

Give yourself an honest score on each dimension and plot it. The dents are your growth list — the shape shows exactly where you're strong and where you're thin, so effort goes to the right place instead of everywhere. Then run the same radar on other people: it's a fast, fair read of who's strong where, and what you can learn from them.

The verdict
Growing or stagnating — in yourself and others

The single most useful signal is direction: is someone growing or coasting? Growth looks like seeking feedback, choosing discomfort, and being curious about being wrong; stagnation looks like defending the ego and calling every flaw "just who I am." Watch for it in yourself first — then in others, because it tells you who's worth growing alongside and who's quietly stuck.

How it's built
Read it → extract the move → practise it live

This is your own learning method pointed at yourself: study how admired people carry themselves — in books, shows, series, real life — extract the specific move (not "be confident" but "he paused before answering"), turn it into a cue, practise it live, and keep what works. Character built deliberately, one move at a time.

Cue — Before speaking: true, useful, right moment? — else hold. Under pressure: use the gap, respond don't react. In conflict: positions → interests, know your walk-away, keep the person. And build every bit of it by extracting moves from what you read and watch, then practising them for real.
Speak to improve the silenceUse the gap: respond, not reactPositions → interestsKnow your BATNACarry it: calm · warm · steadyExtract moves from books & showsPractise live

The takeaway

How you conduct yourself is built, not born. Map the dimensions — presence, speech, composure, negotiation, judgment, self-presentation — and train each like a skill: study it, name the move, internalise it, practise it live. Bearing is just a stack of small, learnable habits worn until they're yours.

A living, visual reference built from first principles of human communication — negotiation, relationships, and behaviour modelling. Every law below carries its research grounding, then an explicit map from the first principle to the best practice it produces — so nothing here is an arbitrary tip.

Standing agreement · working method
Crude models, applied — then refined

Heavy upfront model-building is abandoned in favour of short, crude, loosely-held models built in whatever pockets of time exist: discuss a piece → log a crude cue + action items → apply in real life → return & refine. Why: correction against real application teaches more per minute than a polished model built in isolation — the correction is where understanding sticks.

01 · Communication
Understanding is one layer — not the base
The core claim
Communication is negotiating a shared reality

Mutual understanding is usually treated as the base of communication, with charm, humour and small talk as decoration on top. Flip it: understanding is just the layer we notice consciously. Communication is less about transferring information and more about two people continuously negotiating a shared reality — information transfer is only the visible slice.

The anatomy
Five layers run in every exchange — at once

Every utterance carries all five at once, in different proportions: Attention/Framing (is a communicative act even happening?), Relational Calibration (a claim about status, closeness, stance — the phatic layer), Content Transmission (what we usually call "understanding"), Coordination/Prediction (aligning what happens next), and Self-Presentation/Identity (a display of who you are, independent of content).

Principle 1 · Attention
Don't speak until they've arrived
The law & its roots
Physical presence ≠ attentional presence

This predates language — it's rooted in serial, not parallel, attention: a redirect step is mechanically required before any content can be processed. Opening a channel is a small consent-seeking act; skip it and the message reads as intrusion regardless of content. Cue: "Don't speak until they've arrived." Physical presence ≠ attentional presence.

Why a message fails
Fishbone — six root causes feeding one failure

Before assuming your words were wrong, check the six bones. Most "they didn't get it" failures are actually timing, channel, or the receiver's state — not the content at all.

The diagnostic
The Receptiveness Radar — Timing × Channel × Load

Receptiveness is a product of three axes: Timing (good time in their day/week — macro), Channel (right entry point for this kind of message), and Load (mentally free this instant — micro). Diagnose the axis before you escalate: unread → usually Channel (try a different access point); read, silent, known-free → Timing (wait or ask for a slot); read, silent, channel usually works → Load (shrink the ask or wait it out). Cue: "Diagnose the axis before you escalate." Changing channel + timing + urgency all at once teaches you nothing about what broke.

Principle 2 · Relational framing
Content is decoded through the relationship
The law & the loop
The relational frame is the decoder, run before content registers

Content has no fixed meaning on its own — identical words mean different things depending on relational standing. "Where do I stand with this person" never switches off (coalition membership was survival). Most people manage only the first half of the loop; skill is deliberately using the second half — testing/moving the frame, with a fast read-and-retract if it fails.

The two dials
Content firmness × relational warmth

Firmness and warmth are two independent dials — the classic mistake is collapsing them into one. Firm + Warm ("I need to push back, and I still value working with you") is the highest-skill combination: respected, and it earns loyalty. Keep them separate and you can disagree hard while staying warm.

Goal-directed movement
The MAYA curve + the Walk Loop

To move a relationship, make the most advanced yet acceptable move — one knob, sized correctly. Too safe reads as flat; too advanced gets rejected; the peak is the calibrated single move. Run the Walk Loop: keep probes cheap so a miss costs nothing — then abandon without clinging. Analogy to Berlyne's inverted-U / optimal-stimulation (arousal theory); MAYA coined by Raymond Loewy for design novelty. One-knob discipline is for your moves — so the result is attributable.

The map you read
The Relational Radar — seven axes

Warmth and Status/Dominance are the two indispensable axes; Closeness, Formality, Trust, Reciprocity and Similarity extend them. Grounding: Leary's Interpersonal Circumplex (1957); Fiske, Cuddy & Glick's Stereotype Content Model (2002). Practical rule: Warmth + Status are mandatory; add Closeness + Trust next; pull in the rest only when they matter. (What was first called "Openness" is really Closeness/Intimacy or Formality — not the Big-Five trait.)

Getting it wrong
Failure modes & fixes — and the scope of the radar
Misjudged baseline

Assuming a frame not yet earned. Fix: default to the conservative reading until evidence says otherwise.

Stale frame

Running an outdated reading. Fix: re-measure the radar periodically.

Collapsing the dials

Firmness bleeding into coldness. Fix: choose tone and content as two separate decisions.

Projected frame

Assuming your comfort is the shared default. Fix: read their actual signals and mirror those.

The radar is a thermometer, not a thermostat — it reads the current state; it contains no goal of changing the relationship. Reading it (Principle 2) and deciding to move it (Relationships / Negotiation) are two different decisions. Cue: "Re-measure, don't remember — and don't mistake your own default for theirs."

Principle 3 · Content transfer
You can't see inside another mind
The law & the model
Shannon–Weaver: encode → signal → decode, fresh every time

Minds are causally sealed: content transfer is the only bridge across the gap, and every bridge is built fresh each time. Grounding: Shannon & Weaver's 1949 model. Body language isn't outside this process — it's a second channel of the same transfer (Law 4): words are digital/precise for facts, tone/body analogic/continuous for emotion and emphasis.

The five invariant laws
Everything about content transfer hangs off these
Law 1 · lossy encoding
A high-dimensional thought squeezed into low-dimensional words
Dimensionality collapse

A thought is high-dimensional; language is low-dimensional and serial — projecting one onto the other drops information, like a 2D photo of a 3D scene.

Quantization

Continuous experience rounds into the nearest discrete word — untranslatables (hygge, saudade) prove languages quantize onto different, incomplete grids.

Symbol ≠ thing

Meaning is reconstructed by the listener from their own associations, not transmitted directly (Saussure).

Grounding: Shannon's 1948 Noisy-Channel theorem — every channel has a capacity ceiling; human communication operates deep in the lossy region. Countermeasures (kept distinct): redundancy for random noise · a different representational mode (analogy/story) to carry a lost dimension.

Law 2 · shared context
Meaning needs shared context, not just a shared code

Words are pointers into shared space, not self-contained meaning packets — every utterance is interpreted against whichever layer is actually shared. Proof: Newton's 1990 "tappers & listeners" — tappers estimated 50% would guess the tune; only ~2.5% did.

Law 3 · bandwidth limits
Working memory is ~4 chunks — a hard ceiling
Intrinsic load

Genuine difficulty of the material — not under your control.

Extraneous load

Controllable clutter — attack this first, before assuming the material needs simplifying.

Germane load

Productive effort — the good kind you want to preserve.

Only extraneous load is under the sender's control. Grounding: Miller's "Magical Number Seven" (1956), narrowed by Cowan (2001) to ~4; Sweller's Cognitive Load (1988); serial-position effect (Murdock 1962 — beginnings and ends recalled far better than middles); vigilance decrement (Mackworth 1948). Law 4 · multi-channel signal
Digital words + analogic tone/body, at once

Grounding: Watzlawick, Beavin & Jackson (1967). Myth-correction: Mehrabian's "93% nonverbal" is over-generalised — his actual finding is narrow: when words and tone/face conflict on feelings, listeners weight tone/face more. The double bind (Bateson et al., 1956) is a message that contradicts itself across the two channels.

Law 5 · redundancy
Trade efficiency for reliability — a real trade-off, not a rule

Repetition, restatement and examples increase the odds a message survives distortion, at the cost of time/bandwidth — the same logic as error-correcting codes. This is a genuine trade-off, not a universal "always be brief" or "always over-explain." It's also distinct from diversity-coding: redundancy defends against random noise; using an analogy to carry a different dimension of the idea is Law 1's countermeasure. First principle → repetition raises the odds the true content survives somewhere among redundant copies ⟹ practice: add deliberate redundancy on the key point for high-stakes messages; stay lean on low-stakes ones.

The assembled stack
Master synthesis — an end-to-end protocol

Gate on receptiveness (P1) → read the frame (P2) → assume loss and counter with redundancy and a different mode (Law 1) → verify shared background (Law 2) → keep turns short (Law 3). Two genuine syntheses fall out: shrinking the first/last gap (short units have no erasable middle) and turn-taking as one mechanism — capping a turn at 3–4 minutes.

Cross-cutting failure
Flooding breaks the whole stack under conflict

High-arousal conflict is a distinct causal regime — the turn-cap needs the exact executive control that conflict disables. Grounding: Gottman's flooding (HR >~100 bpm) = the amygdala-hijack mechanism (LeDoux/Goleman). Structures, not willpower: a pause signal pre-agreed in calm (never argued with when invoked); a hard 20-minute floor (Gottman — long enough for cortisol/adrenaline to clear); the Speaker–Listener Technique (Markman & Stanley, PREP). Cue: "Flooded brains can't follow rules — only structures."

05 · Behaviour modelling
Why "right now" is easy and "next Tuesday" isn't
Optionality anxiety
Structural, not personal — and it scales up with a fuller life

The reluctance to commit to "next Tuesday" is structural, not personal — named optionality anxiety / FOBO, the future-facing sibling of FOMO. It's loss aversion on time: giving away an open slot's potential feels worse than the known gain, even when the odds of something better are low. Symmetry: when others hesitate to commit to you, it's their uncertainty-management, not a signal about the relationship. Critique of "always pre-commit": blind full commitment overcommits you — the skill is calibrated commitment, not maximal.

One-knob escalation — the heart of it

This single loop is the engine of the whole thing — and it's what keeps it respectful by design. Move one knob (a warmer compliment, a tease, a brief touch, sitting closer, holding eye contact a beat longer), then read whether she returns it. Returned → advance one more notch; not returned → retract gracefully, stay warm, no pressure. Because you only ever advance where she meets you, consent is built into the mechanic. This is the MAYA / Walk-Loop from the Relational Framing section, in romantic mode.

The tools
What you actually do — and the levers
Actions → axis
Every move raises one intimacy or desire axis

Same meta-rule as the general stack: no separate "first principle of actions" — every move is chosen to raise one axis. Notice how it splits: grooming, teasing, novel dates, non-neediness, touch feed the desire engine; genuine listening, remembering, vulnerability feed the intimacy engine. A great courtship keeps both columns moving — that's the Perel balance in practice.

The attraction levers
How desirability is raised — ethically
Who you are

Confidence & calm (non-neediness) · status/competence · a full life she's joining, not being rescued by.

Social signal

Pre-selection (others enjoy you) · being well-regarded · warmth toward everyone, not just her.

In the moment

Playful tension · a little mystery (don't over-share) · shared novelty/adventure · escalating touch.

These are the romantic version of the influence levers — ways to genuinely raise desirability, not tricks. The ethical line is the same as everywhere in the library: build real attraction and read real signals; the moment it becomes pressure, false scarcity, or manipulation, it stops being attraction and curdles. The strongest lever by far is the boring one — be someone with a genuinely good life and calm confidence, and let her opt in.

The part everyone skips
Keep — love and desire, on purpose

Getting together is the start, not the finish. Long relationships die from neglecting one of the two engines — either the bond rots (contempt, no repair) or the desire flatlines (all comfort, no novelty). The maintenance work is to protect both on purpose, knowing they pull against each other: Gottman's antidotes for love, and Perel's "air" — novelty, adventure, space — for desire.

The blueprint, one line — Presence to get in → run the lifecycle (spot → approach → open → spark → escalate → close → date → intimacy → define → maintain) → feeding both engines the whole way (intimacy actions and desire actions) → one-knob, reciprocity-checked escalation throughout → then protect love and desire deliberately for the long run.

The takeaway

This is your romantic operating system: a desire radar (the first principles of the charge), a lifecycle (the moves from first sight to together), an actions→axis map (what to actually do), and the maintenance layer. It runs on the same machinery as the general stack — presence, lifecycle, drivers — with a second engine (desire) running in parallel and a consent-respecting escalation built into every step. Learn it, then we drill it: reading signals, approaches, escalation calibration, and the awkward moments, until the fear drops and it's just moves.
